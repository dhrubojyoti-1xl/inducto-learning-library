Examples & Structured Prompts

Showing one good example beats three paragraphs describing what good looks like.

Why this matters

Aditya explains the format four times

Aditya prepares payment summaries for a Mumbai finance team. He describes the format he wants in three sentences. The answer comes back close but wrong. He describes it again. And again. On the fourth try he pastes one finished example instead.

Twelve minutes of describing, when showing took twenty seconds.
What changes

One worked example does what a paragraph of description cannot.

Show, do not describe

Why one example wins

Why one example wins

Describing a format is a translation step. An example removes it entirely.

1

You describe a format

Three sentences about columns and order.

2

It interprets them

Its reading of your words, not your picture.

3

You correct it

Twice, usually, and it drifts back.

4

Or you show one

The pattern is now unambiguous.

Terms on this page: Few-shot

Describing versus showing

Described
  • "Give me a short summary line for each invoice, with the amount at the end in brackets."
  • You get full sentences, amounts in the middle, and a heading you did not ask for.
  • Three rounds of correction later it is nearly right.
Shown
  • "Follow this exact pattern: Ashirwad Traders — 45 days overdue, second reminder sent (₹2,40,000)"
  • You get twelve lines in that shape, first time.
  • Nothing to correct, because nothing was open to interpretation.

An example is not a hint. It is the specification, written in the only language that cannot be misread.

One example, done well

The worked pattern

The one-example prompt

Copy this prompt
Rewrite each line below to follow this exact pattern, and nothing else: Ashirwad Traders — 45 days overdue, second reminder sent (₹2,40,000). Keep the same punctuation, the same order and the brackets. No heading, no introduction, one line per entry.
Then paste your messy lines underneath it.
Why this works
  • The example fixes order, punctuation and bracket style at once.
  • "No heading, no introduction" stops the usual additions.
  • You can check twelve lines against one pattern in seconds.

What makes an example work

Two examples fix tone

What description cannot reach

Two examples fix a tone

Tone is the thing people describe worst and recognise instantly. Show it twice.

Copy this two-example prompt
Match the tone of these two examples exactly. Example one: "Thanks — noted. I will confirm the revised date by Thursday." Example two: "Understood. We will cover the freight difference on this consignment." Now write a reply to the message below, in that same tone.
Short, warm, no padding. Far easier to show than to describe.
What comes back
  • A reply in the same clipped, decisive register as your two examples.
  • No "I hope this finds you well", because neither example had one.
  • Tone matched without you ever finding a word for it.

How many examples to give

One example

Fixes structure

  • Layout, order and punctuation
  • Field names and their sequence
  • Line length and bracket style
  • Enough for most formatting jobs
Two or three

Fixes tone and judgement

  • Register — clipped, warm, formal
  • What to include and what to drop
  • How to handle an awkward case
  • Worth it for anything customer-facing
Fill-in-the-blanks

Structure that cannot drift

Structure that cannot drift

Copy this structured prompt
Fill in this exact structure and change nothing else. SITUATION: one sentence. WHAT CHANGED: one sentence. IMPACT ON THE CUSTOMER: one sentence. WHAT WE ARE DOING: two sentences. NEXT UPDATE: a date. Use only the facts below and write [CHECK] where a fact is missing.
Headings in capitals are followed far more reliably.
Why this works
  • The shape is fixed, so every answer is comparable.
  • [CHECK] makes a missing fact visible instead of invented.
  • Anyone on the team can read it the same way.

Where structure pays off most

Anything repeated

A weekly update, a site report, a delay note. The shape stops being a decision every time.

Anything compared

Five supplier assessments in the same five fields can actually be read side by side.

Anything handed over

A colleague picking it up knows exactly where each piece of information lives.

Do this now

Turn a repeat job into a template

Do this now: build one template

  1. Find a document you produce more than once a month.
  2. Take the best one you have ever written and open it.
  3. Paste it in with the prompt on the right.
  4. Save what comes back. That is your template from now on.
Copy this prompt
Below is a good example of a document I write regularly. Turn it into a reusable template: keep the structure and the headings exactly, and replace every specific fact with a clearly marked bracket saying what belongs there. Do not change the wording around the brackets.
Your own best work becomes the specification.

Never put these in an example

An example is pasted text. Everything in it leaves the company with it.

Use a realistic invented case, or strip the real one first.

  • A real customer's name, site or contact details
  • Real prices, margins or contract wording
  • A colleague's name beside performance or pay
  • Account numbers, references or internal system names

Four habits with examples

Five mistakes people really make

1
Describing a format in three sentences
You are asking it to imagine your picture. Showing the picture takes twenty seconds.
2
Giving a half-finished example
Every gap in the example becomes a decision it makes for you, differently each time.
3
Pasting a real customer record as the example
The format was the point. The name, address and amount went with it.
4
Using five examples when one would do
Long prompts dilute the instruction. One complete example beats five partial ones.
5
Forgetting "nothing else"
You get your format plus a heading, an introduction and a closing line.

Describe or show?

Could I show what I want in under five lines?
Yes

Show it

Layouts, tone, line formats, table shapes. An example is shorter than the description and cannot be misread.

No

Describe it

Long documents and complex judgements. Describe the structure in capitals, then show one section as an example.

The example rule

If you have explained the format twice, stop explaining and paste an example.

The second explanation is always slower than the example would have been.

  • One example — structure fixed.
  • Two examples — tone fixed.
  • Zero examples — you will explain it again.

Recap

Examples on one screen

Show, do not describe

A description must be interpreted. An example cannot be misread.

One example fixes structure

Layout, order, punctuation and bracket style, all in one line.

Two examples fix tone

Register is easy to recognise and almost impossible to describe.

Always add "nothing else"

Otherwise you get your format plus a heading and an introduction.

Use capitals for structure

SITUATION, IMPACT, NEXT UPDATE. Headings in capitals get followed.

Never show a real record

The format works with invented data. A real one leaks a real customer.

If you have explained the format twice, stop explaining and paste an example.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Few-shot
Giving one or two worked examples before your request, so the pattern is shown rather than described.
Zero-shot
Asking without any example. Fine for simple jobs, weak for formats and tone.
Template
A reusable prompt with brackets where the specifics go.
Structured prompt
A prompt with fixed headings the answer must fill in, so every result is comparable.
Prompt
Everything you type in: context plus instructions plus your facts.
Output
What comes back. Your draft, which you are responsible for checking.
