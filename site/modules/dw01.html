Email Writing with AI

Thirty emails a day, written in a third of the time, without sounding like a robot wrote them.

Why this matters

Imran writes thirty emails a day

Imran coordinates logistics from Sharjah. Most of his day is emails: chasing a truck, explaining a delay, refusing a discount, confirming a slot. He knows what to say every time. Finding the words is what takes the day.

Roughly two and a half hours a day, on wording alone.
What changes

Four email shapes, ready to paste, that cover most of a week.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Draft any routine work email in under 60 seconds
  • 2Set a tone deliberately instead of hoping it lands right
  • 3Turn a long thread into a reply without reading it twice
  • 4Write about a customer without ever naming them
  • 5Keep four email prompts you will use every week
The four email shapes

What most of your week is

Four shapes cover your week

Almost every routine work email is one of these. Each has a prompt you fill in once and reuse.

What every shape needs

1

The facts

Order number, dates, amounts, the actual reason.

2

Your position

What you will and will not offer.

3

The tone

Firm, warm or neutral. Choose it on purpose.

4

The ask

One clear thing you want them to do next.

Setting the tone

Firm, warm, or neutral

Choose the tone on purpose

Vague tone

"Make it professional"

  • Produces the same neutral letter every time
  • Over-apologises when you did nothing wrong
  • Softens a refusal until it reads as a maybe
  • Adds pleasantries nobody reads
Named tone

"Firm, no apology, warm close"

  • Firm — states the position without hedging
  • Warm — acknowledges the person, not the problem
  • Neutral — facts only, for a record
  • Apologetic — once, specifically, then move on

The bad-news email prompt

Copy this prompt
Write an email telling a customer their delivery has slipped. Facts: promised 12 March, now 15 March, delayed by a transport strike, no cost to them. Tone: apologetic once and then factual, not grovelling. Under 110 words. End by confirming the new date. No opening pleasantries.
Works as written. Swap in your own dates and reason.
Why this works
  • "Apologetic once" stops the triple apology.
  • "No opening pleasantries" removes forty wasted words.
  • Ending on the new date is what the customer wants first.
Replying to a long thread

Without reading it twice

The twelve-message thread

You have been added to a thread with a decision buried in it. You have four minutes.

Copy this thread prompt
Below is an email thread. Tell me three things and nothing else. One: what has actually been agreed. Two: what is still open. Three: what I am being asked to do. Use one short line for each. Do not summarise the whole conversation.
Strip the names out of the thread before you paste it.
What comes back
  • Three short lines instead of twelve messages.
  • The open question you would have missed on a fast read.
  • Four minutes of reading turned into forty seconds.

Strip it before you paste it

A pasted thread carries every name, address and signature block in it.

Describe the situation, or delete the identifying lines first.

  • Signature blocks with phone numbers and addresses
  • Customer names in every quoted reply
  • Internal comments colleagues added along the way
  • Attachments, prices and account references
The subject line

Where most emails fail

Where most emails fail

Vague subject
  • Subject: Update
  • Sits unopened for two days in a full inbox.
  • When it is opened, nobody knows what is being asked.
Specific subject
  • Subject: Order 4471 — new delivery date 15 March, no action needed
  • The whole message is readable without opening it.
  • It gets filed correctly and found again in June.

A good subject line says what it is about and whether the reader has to do anything.

Ask for ten, keep one

Copy this subject-line prompt
Give me ten subject lines for the email below. Each under nine words. Each must say what it is about and whether the reader needs to act. Number them. No explanation and no commentary.
Choosing from ten takes ten seconds. Inventing one takes two minutes.
Why this works
  • Ten options cost the same as one.
  • "Whether the reader needs to act" is the useful half.
  • Numbered lines make picking one instant.
Do this now

Write a real one

Do this now: write a real one

  1. Open the email you have been putting off since yesterday.
  2. Write down the facts, your position, the tone and the ask.
  3. Paste the prompt on the right with those four things in it.
  4. Check the dates, add the name yourself in Outlook, send.
Copy this prompt
Write a work email. Facts: [dates, numbers, what happened]. My position: [what I will and will not offer]. Tone: [firm / warm / neutral], one apology at most. Under [number] words. End with one clear ask. No opening pleasantries, no restating my request.
Four brackets. This is the only email prompt most people need.

Four habits that keep it human

Five mistakes people really make

1
Pasting the customer's email to "give context"
Their name, address and phone number leave the company to save you thirty seconds.
2
Sending it without reading the middle
The invented commitment is never in the first line or the last.
3
Asking for "a professional email"
You get the same neutral letter every time, and it matches no situation exactly.
4
Letting it apologise three times
A triple apology reads as an admission. Say "one apology at most".
5
Keeping the AI subject line unread
"Update Regarding Your Recent Order" is how an email goes unopened for two days.

When not to use it at all

Would I want this read out in a dispute?
Yes

Write it yourself

Disciplinary matters, contract commitments, anything about a person. Use the tool to check the wording afterwards, not to compose it.

No

Draft it with AI

Delays, confirmations, chasers, routine refusals. This is most of your inbox, and it is exactly where the time goes.

What good use looks like

Sixty seconds

Facts in, draft out, dates checked, sent. Not five minutes of staring at a blank reply.

Tone you chose

Firm when firm is right, warm when it is not. Decided on purpose rather than by mood.

Still sounds like you

Three words changed and your greeting added is usually all it takes.

The email rule

You supply the facts, the position and the ask. It supplies the sentences.

Every good AI email comes from that split. Every bad one comes from skipping it.

  • Facts and position — yours.
  • Wording and tone — its job.
  • The final read — always yours.

Recap

Email with AI on one screen

Four shapes cover the week

Bad news, chasing, refusing, confirming. One prompt each.

Facts, position, tone, ask

Give it all four and the first draft is usually sendable.

Name the tone, cap the apologies

"Firm, one apology at most" beats "make it professional".

Never paste the customer's email

Describe the situation instead. The reply is just as good.

Subject lines carry the message

Topic plus whether they must act. Ask for ten, keep one.

Read the middle before sending

Openings and closings get checked. Middles carry the invented line.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Tone
The register of a message: firm, warm, neutral, apologetic. Name it rather than hoping.
Thread
An email conversation with replies stacked inside it. Full of names and signature blocks.
Prompt
Everything you type in: the facts, your position, the tone and the ask.
Template
A prompt with brackets where the specifics go, so you reuse it without rewriting.
Personal data
Anything identifying a person: a name, a phone number, an address, an account.
Output
What comes back. Your draft, which you are responsible for checking.
