Basic Prompting

The first prompt you ever write — and the six-part shape that makes it work every time, not just when you get lucky.

Why this matters

Anjali types four words and gets junk

Anjali handles client escalations for a Dubai office. She types "write reply to angry customer" and gets a bland three-paragraph letter with no order number and no promise in it. She deletes it and writes the email herself, the way she always has.

Twenty minutes lost — and a quiet decision that the tool does not work.
What changes

Six short parts, in one paragraph. The first draft comes back usable instead of generic.

Why prompts fail

The four-word problem

Why "write an email" fails

The tool is not guessing badly. It is guessing — because you left it nothing else to do.

What you typed

write reply to angry customer

  • No order number, so it cannot mention one
  • No reason, so it apologises for nothing in particular
  • No length, so you get six paragraphs
  • No tone, so it sounds like a form letter
What it had to invent

Everything else

  • Who the customer is and what they bought
  • What actually went wrong, and whose fault it was
  • What you are willing to promise them
  • Whether this is an email, a WhatsApp or a call note

A prompt is a briefing

Think of it as briefing a capable temp on their first morning. They will do exactly what you describe, and invent the rest.

1

You brief it

Task, context, facts, format — in plain sentences.

2

It fills the gaps

Anything you left out, it invents a reasonable version of.

3

You get a draft

Specific if you were specific. Generic if you were vague.

4

You check and send

Two minutes of checking, not twenty of rewriting.

The six parts

The shape of every good prompt

The six parts of a good prompt

Say these six things and there is almost nothing left for the tool to guess.

The six parts, in one paragraph

You do not need headings or bullet points. Write it the way you would say it out loud.

Copy this prompt
Write a reply email to a client whose delivery slipped. Order 4471 was promised on 12 March and will now arrive on 15 March, delayed by a transport strike. Tone: apologetic but calm. Under 120 words. Give me a subject line too.
Six parts, one paragraph, 42 words. It works as written.
Why this works
  • Every gap is filled, so nothing has to be invented.
  • Each fact in the reply can be checked against your prompt.
  • No headings needed — plain sentences are enough.
Build one now

Do it live, with your own email

Build one now, with your own work

  1. Pick a real message you actually have to send today.
  2. Say the six parts out loud first. It takes thirty seconds.
  3. Type them as one paragraph. Do not format it, do not use bullet points.
  4. Read the reply and count how many of your facts it used.
Copy this prompt
Write a short WhatsApp message to a supplier chasing a pending delivery. PO 2290 was due on 8 April and has not arrived. This is the second time we have followed up. Tone: firm but polite. Under 60 words. Just the message, no greeting block.
Try this one first, then rewrite it with your own PO number.

Four words versus six parts

Four words
  • You type: write reply to angry customer
  • You get a letter that apologises for "the inconvenience" three times and names nothing at all.
  • You delete it and write the email yourself. Twenty minutes gone.
Six parts
  • You type the six-part paragraph from two slides back.
  • You get a 110-word reply carrying the order number, both dates and one clear apology.
  • You check the dates, change one word, and send. Three minutes.

Same tool, same afternoon. The difference was thirty seconds of typing.

Set the output

Length, tone and format

Length, tone and format do the work

Length

"Under 120 words." "Exactly five bullets." "Two sentences." Always say a number.

Tone

"Apologetic but calm." "Direct, no small talk." "Warm — this client has been with us nine years."

Format

"A table with three columns." "An email with a subject line." "Five bullets, no introduction."

Ask for a table, get a table

Copy this prompt
Turn this into a table with three columns: item, problem, who fixes it. One row per item and no extra commentary. Items: printer on the 2nd floor jams every third print; meeting room AC has not cooled since Monday; visitor wifi password is not working for guests.
"No extra commentary" is the part people forget.
What comes back
  • A clean three-column table with three rows, and nothing else around it.
  • No "Here is your table", no closing sentence — because you asked for no extra commentary.
  • You can paste it straight into an email or a Word file without deleting anything.

Words that make a prompt worse

1
"Make it professional"
It cannot tell what that means to you. Say "no slang, no exclamation marks, full sentences".
2
"Make it better"
Better than what? Name the change: shorter, warmer, fewer numbers, one apology instead of three.
3
"Urgent" and "ASAP"
The tool has no clock and no queue. You spent a word on urgency instead of on the order number.
4
"...and so on"
It will invent the rest of your list. Write out every item you actually want covered.
5
"Summarise this" with nothing attached
There is nothing to summarise, so you get a general paragraph about the topic. Paste the text first.
Fix a weak answer

One follow-up, not a restart

One follow-up beats a restart

Copy this follow-up
That is close. Keep the first two paragraphs exactly as they are. Replace the last paragraph with one line confirming the new delivery date of 15 March. Cut the whole thing to under 100 words.
Protect, then change, then give the new fact.
Why this works
  • It keeps the part that already worked.
  • It names the change, so nothing else moves.
  • It supplies the new fact, so nothing gets invented.

Three follow-ups that always work

Cut it down

"Half the length, same facts, keep both dates." A target plus something to protect.

Change the reader

"Rewrite this for a client who is already annoyed with us." The facts stay, the tone moves.

Ask for options

"Give me three versions, numbered, and say what is different about each."

When not to bother prompting

Is the answer already written down somewhere?
Yes

Go and get it

The GST rate, a clause in the contract, last month's dispatch number. Opening the file takes less time than checking an answer that might be invented.

No

Write the prompt

You have the facts in your head or on the screen in front of you, and what you actually need is the wording. This is exactly what prompting is for.

Keep your best prompts

The people who get the most out of these tools are not the fastest typists. They are the ones with ten good prompts saved.

A prompt you can reuse tomorrow

Your reusable template
Write a [type of message] to [who it is for]. Situation: [what happened, in one sentence]. Facts: [the dates, numbers and names you are willing to share]. Tone: [how it should sound]. Length: under [number] words. Format: [email with a subject line / plain message / bullets].
Six brackets. That is the whole method.
Filled in, it looks like this
  • "Write a WhatsApp message to our transporter. Situation: the 8 April pickup did not happen."
  • "Facts: PO 2290, due 8 April, second follow-up. Tone: firm but polite. Length: under 60 words. Format: plain message."
  • Thirty seconds of filling in, and a message you can send.

Never put these in a prompt

A prompt leaves your laptop. Write it as if it will be read by someone outside the company.

Because in most cases it is — the tool runs on somebody else's servers.

  • Customer names, phone numbers or addresses
  • Prices, margins or anything lifted from a contract
  • A colleague's name attached to pay or performance

Recap

Basic Prompting on one screen

Six parts, one paragraph

Task, context, facts, tone, length, format — in plain sentences.

The facts are your job

Dates, numbers and names are the part only you have. Leave them out and they get invented.

Say the number

"Under 100 words." "Exactly five bullets." "Three columns." Vague in, vague out.

Adjectives do nothing

"Professional", "better", "urgent" give it nothing to act on. Name the actual change.

Correct, don't restart

"Keep the first two paragraphs. Cut to 80 words." One line saves the whole draft.

Save the shape, not the details

Store the prompt with brackets where the customer data used to be.

A prompt is a briefing. Everything you leave out gets filled in for you — and you may not notice which parts.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Prompt
Everything you type in: the request plus the facts you choose to supply.
Context
The background you give so the tool knows who the text is for and why it is being written.
Token
A small piece of text, roughly three quarters of a word. Length limits are counted in tokens, not letters.
Zero-shot
Asking for something without showing an example first. Most everyday prompts are zero-shot.
Few-shot
Giving one or two examples of what good looks like before you make your request.
Output
What comes back. A draft you are responsible for, never a source you can quote.
