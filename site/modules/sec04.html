Data Protection Basics

What counts as personal data, what the law expects, and what that means for your inbox on an ordinary Tuesday.

Why this matters

Manoj forwards a list to his own email

Manoj handles sales admin in Kochi. He wants to finish a report at home, so he forwards the customer contact list to his personal email. Nothing bad happens. It is still a reportable transfer of personal data out of the company.

A breach on paper, with no attacker and no bad intention.
What changes

Four rules that cover almost every decision you will face.

What counts as personal data

Wider than you think

Wider than most people think

If it points to one identifiable person, it is personal data. A name is not required.

Obviously personal

Everyone gets these right

  • Name, address and phone number
  • Email address and date of birth
  • Passport, Emirates ID or Aadhaar number
  • Salary, bank details and appraisal notes
Also personal

The ones people miss

  • A vehicle number plate on a gate pass
  • A photo of a person, or CCTV footage
  • An employee number with no name attached
  • A delivery address on its own

The four rules

Enough for most decisions

Four rules, most decisions

Collect less is the strongest

Collecting everything
  • A visitor form asking for address, ID number and vehicle details.
  • Kept in a folder for three years "in case".
  • Every one of those records is a liability with no purpose.
Collecting what is needed
  • Name, company, who they are visiting, time in and out.
  • Kept for the period we actually need, then destroyed.
  • Nothing held that we could not explain a reason for.

Data you never collected cannot leak, cannot be requested and cannot be stolen. It is the only perfect protection there is.

Everyday breaches

No attacker required

No attacker required

1
Forwarding a list to your personal email
Personal data has left our systems for one we cannot audit, secure or delete.
2
Using CC instead of BCC on a customer mailout
Every recipient now has every other customer's email address.
3
Leaving a printout on the shared printer
Anyone walking past has it. This is one of the most common reported incidents.
4
Keeping a spreadsheet from a project that ended in 2023
Still personal data, still our responsibility, and nobody remembers it exists.
5
Sending a file to the wrong Rajesh
Autocomplete picks the wrong contact. It happens weekly somewhere in every company.

The BCC habit

One CC field on one mailout exposes every customer address to every other customer.

It is the single most common accidental disclosure there is.

  • More than one external recipient — use BCC.
  • Check the field before every send.
  • If in doubt, send them separately.
When it goes wrong

Reporting and the clock

The clock starts when you know

Notification deadlines are measured from the moment somebody in the company becomes aware. Not from when harm appears.

1

Something goes wrong

A wrong recipient, a lost laptop, a bad paste.

2

You become aware

The clock starts here, at this moment.

3

You report it

Same day, to the named contact, with what you know.

4

They assess and notify

Within the statutory window, if it qualifies.

The report, ready to send

Copy this wording
I need to report a possible data incident. What happened: [one sentence]. When: [date and approximate time]. What data was involved: [type and rough number of people]. Who else knows: [names]. I have not deleted anything. Please tell me what you need from me next.
Send it the same day. Incomplete and fast beats complete and late.
Why this works
  • "I have not deleted anything" matters — evidence is needed.
  • A rough number is enough to start an assessment.
  • It ends with a question, so somebody has to respond.
Do this now

Audit your own inbox

Do this now: audit your inbox

  1. Search your sent items for attachments sent outside the company.
  2. Search your personal email for anything work-related.
  3. Delete work files from any personal account you find them in.
  4. Find one old spreadsheet of personal data and ask whether we still need it.
Copy this prompt
I want to check whether a task involves personal data. Ask me five short questions, one at a time, then tell me whether it does and which of the four handling rules apply. Do not ask me to paste any actual data.
Describe the task. Never paste the records themselves.

When a customer asks what we hold

Where data is allowed to live

Is this a company system somebody administers?
Yes

Fine

Company email, the approved drive, the ERP, the CRM. Backed up, access-controlled, and deletable if we are ever asked.

No

Not allowed

Personal email, a personal cloud drive, a USB stick, WhatsApp, an AI chat window. We cannot secure it, audit it or delete it.

Four habits worth having

The data protection rule

Collect less, keep it inside, delete it when the job is done.

Three habits that cover the overwhelming majority of what either law expects of you.

  • Not collected — cannot leak.
  • Inside our systems — can be protected.
  • Deleted when done — no longer a risk.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Personal data
Any information pointing to one identifiable person, with or without a name attached.
DPDP Act
India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, covering consent, purpose and breach notification.
Data minimisation
Collecting only what you actually need. The strongest protection available.
Data breach
Personal data going somewhere it should not. No attacker is required.
Subject access request
A person asking what data we hold about them. It has a legal response period.
Retention
How long we keep something. Old copies are the ones that cause incidents.
