Presentation Skills

Standing up and saying it — including when you are interrupted on slide three.

Why this matters

Rohit reads his slides out loud

Rohit presents the monthly review in Vadodara. He reads each slide aloud, in order, without stopping. Halfway through, two people are on their phones. He has all the right information and nobody is receiving it.

Forty minutes of the right facts, landing on nobody.
What changes

Say the point, then show the evidence. Never the other way round.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Open with the conclusion instead of the agenda
  • 2Handle an interruption without losing your thread
  • 3Say the point of a slide before showing its detail
  • 4Answer a question you do not know the answer to
  • 5Finish early, deliberately, and be thanked for it
Point before evidence

Why reading fails

Point first, evidence second

If people do not know what a slide is for, they read it instead of listening to you.

Evidence first
  • "This chart shows dispatches by week for the quarter."
  • The room reads the chart while you are still talking.
  • By the time you reach the point, they have formed their own.
Point first
  • "Volumes held up. The problem is rejects, not output."
  • "You can see it in weeks six and seven here."
  • They now look at the chart for the thing you named.

A room reads faster than you speak. Give them the point before they build their own.

Never read the slide

They read it faster

Reading aloud is slower than reading silently. You are holding the room back.

Say what is not on it

The slide carries the data. You carry the meaning, the caveat and the recommendation.

One point per slide

If you need three sentences to say what a slide shows, it is three slides.

The first ninety seconds

You get one opening

You get one opening

Copy this opening
"Three things today. One: volumes held, so capacity is not our problem. Two: rejects doubled in weeks six and seven and I know why. Three: I need a decision on the film supplier by Friday. I will take questions as we go."
Under thirty seconds, and the room knows the whole shape.
Why this works
  • Naming three things tells people when it will end.
  • The decision is stated at the start, not discovered at the end.
  • "Questions as we go" prevents the awkward held hand.

What not to open with

The first thirty seconds are the only ones where you have everybody. Do not spend them on housekeeping.

Agendas, apologies and background all belong later, or nowhere.

  • "Let me start with a bit of background"
  • "Apologies, I only got this together this morning"
  • "I know we are short on time so I will be quick"
  • An agenda slide nobody has ever needed
Being interrupted

It is normal, not an attack

Interruptions are normal

Being stopped on slide three usually means somebody is engaged. It is not an attack, and it is not a failure.

Finding your place again

1

Finish the answer

Do not trail off looking at the screen.

2

Name where you are

"So — back to rejects."

3

Restate the point

One sentence. It helps you and the room.

4

Carry on

Nobody remembers the pause. They remember the fluster.

Questions you cannot answer

Three honest options

Three honest options

What people do

Guessing under pressure

  • An invented figure, stated confidently
  • A vague answer nobody can act on
  • "I think it was around..."
  • It gets quoted back to you in six weeks
What works

Three honest answers

  • "I do not know — I will find out by Thursday."
  • "I have a rough figure but I would not quote it."
  • "That is outside what I looked at. Ops would know."
  • Nobody has ever lost credibility this way

The commitment matters

Copy this wording
"I do not have that to hand and I would rather not guess at it. I will check and come back to you by Thursday." Then write it down, in the room, where they can see you writing it.
Writing it down in front of them is the half people forget.
Why this works
  • Saying you would rather not guess is respected, not penalised.
  • A date turns a non-answer into a commitment.
  • Visibly writing it means they stop worrying you will forget.
Do this now

Rehearse one opening

Do this now: rehearse one opening

  1. Take the next presentation you owe anybody.
  2. Write your opening as three numbered things.
  3. Say it out loud, standing up, timed. Aim under thirty seconds.
  4. Say it again. The second attempt is always the one you use.
Copy this prompt
Turn my three main points into a spoken opening of under 70 words. Number them out loud. State the decision I need at the end. Write it the way somebody would actually say it, not the way it would be written.
Read it aloud. If you stumble, the sentence is too long.

Finishing early is a gift

Have I made my three points and got my decision?
Yes

Stop there

"That is everything from me — we have ten minutes back." Nobody has ever resented this, and it is remembered far more warmly than a thorough overrun.

No

Say what is left

"We are short on time — the one thing I still need is the supplier decision." Protect the decision, drop the detail.

Four habits worth having

Five mistakes people really make

1
Reading the slides aloud
The room reads faster than you speak. You are holding them back and they know it.
2
Apologising in the opening
It tells the room to expect something poor, before they have judged for themselves.
3
Guessing a number under pressure
It gets written down and quoted back six weeks later, with your name on it.
4
Treating an interruption as an attack
It is usually engagement. The fluster costs more than the question did.
5
Running over to cover every slide
The decision you needed is on the last slide nobody reached.

Nerves are not the problem

Nobody can see them

Almost everything you feel is invisible from four metres away.

The first minute is worst

Which is exactly why the opening is the part worth rehearsing out loud.

Preparation beats confidence

A rehearsed opening and three known points do more than any amount of feeling calm.

The presentation rule

Say the point, show the evidence, and stop when you have your decision.

Three habits that separate a presentation people act on from one they sit through.

  • Point before evidence.
  • Three things, numbered, up front.
  • Stop early, on purpose.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Point-first
Stating what a slide means before showing its detail.
Signposting
Telling the room where you are, especially after an interruption.
Parking
Agreeing to take a question outside the meeting, with a date attached.
Appendix
Slides kept after the end for questions you might be asked.
Opening
The first thirty seconds. The only moment you have everybody's attention.
Overrun
Going past the finish time. It costs the decision you came for.
