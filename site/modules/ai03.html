AI Capabilities

The tasks where these tools genuinely save you an hour — and how to spot one on your own desk.

Why this matters

Deepa loses every Monday to formatting

Deepa prepares the MIS pack for a Chennai office. Every Monday she pastes the same 40 rows into a document, rewrites the headings, and turns the numbers into sentences for the management summary. The analysis takes twenty minutes. The formatting takes three hours.

Three hours a week on shape, not on the numbers themselves.
What changes

The shape work is exactly what these tools do best.

The four job shapes

What it is actually good at

Four shapes it handles well

Almost every useful AI task at work is one of these four. None of them require it to know anything.

What they have in common

1

You already hold the facts

Nothing has to be looked up by the tool.

2

The work is language

Wording, ordering, length, tone, layout.

3

You can check it fast

Every line traces back to something you supplied.

4

The risk is low

The worst case is a clumsy draft, not a wrong fact.

Turning shape into time

The weekly jobs it removes

The weekly jobs it removes

The recurring write-up

A weekly delay note, a monthly summary, a site visit report. Same shape, new facts each time.

The reformat

Rows into prose. Prose into a table. A long thread into a decision list.

The awkward reply

Chasing a payment, refusing a request, apologising without accepting blame.

Deepa's three hours, in one prompt

Copy this prompt
Turn these figures into a 150-word management summary. State the three biggest movements and nothing else. Do not add causes I have not given you. Figures: dispatches 412 against a plan of 460; rejections 2.1 per cent against 1.4 last month; overtime hours 380 against 250.
Paste it as written, then swap in your own figures.
What comes back
  • A 150-word summary naming the three movements, in the order you listed them.
  • No invented causes, because you told it not to add any.
  • Three hours of formatting becomes ten minutes of checking.
Getting unstuck

When you cannot start

When you cannot start

A blank page costs more time than a bad draft ever will. This is the cheapest use of all.

Copy this unblocking prompt
I have to write a proposal for a client who wants faster delivery at the same price. I do not know how to structure it. Give me a five-part outline with one line describing what goes in each part. Do not write the proposal itself.
Ask for the structure first. Write the content yourself.
Why this works
  • An outline is judgement you can check in seconds.
  • It cannot invent facts if you asked only for headings.
  • You start writing from part two, not from nothing.

Three unblocking moves

Ask for a structure

"Give me a five-part outline." You keep every decision that matters.

Ask for the questions

"What should I ask this supplier before I agree?" A checklist beats a blank page.

Ask for ten, keep one

"Ten subject lines for this email." Choosing is far faster than inventing.

Spot one on your desk

A ten-second test

The ten-second test

Do I already know everything the answer needs?
Yes

Good fit

You are asking for language, not for knowledge. Give it your facts, set the length and tone, and check the draft against what you typed.

No

Poor fit

You are asking it to supply a fact it has no access to. It will produce one anyway. Go to the system, the file or the person instead.

Do this now: find one job

  1. Look at your calendar for last week.
  2. Find one task you did that was mostly typing, not deciding.
  3. Write down the facts you had before you started it.
  4. Paste the prompt on the right, with those facts in it.
Copy this prompt
I am going to give you facts and I want you to write the document. Do not add anything I have not told you. If something important is missing, list the questions at the end instead of guessing. Here are the facts:
The last sentence is what stops it inventing. Keep it in every time.
Where the value stops

Fits that are not fits

Fits that are not fits

Looks like a fit

But it is not

  • "Summarise our contract with this supplier"
  • "What is the current rate for this material?"
  • "Is this clause enforceable in the UAE?"
  • "How many units did we ship last month?"
Actually a fit

Same job, reframed

  • "Draft questions to ask before I sign this"
  • "Word this rate request to the supplier"
  • "List what I should ask our lawyer"
  • "Turn these shipment figures into a summary"

The reframe that rescues a task

Most poor-fit tasks become good-fit tasks if you move the knowledge back to your side.

Asking it to know
  • "What does our late-delivery penalty clause say?"
  • It produces a clause. The clause sounds standard and reasonable.
  • It is not your clause, and nothing on screen tells you that.
Asking it to write
  • You open the contract and read the clause yourself.
  • "Explain this clause in plain English for a customer."
  • You get a clear explanation of the words you actually pasted.

Move the knowing to your side of the desk. Leave the wording on its side.

Four checks before you rely on it

Five mistakes people really make

1
Using it as a search engine
It answers rather than admitting it cannot look things up. The answer looks identical either way.
2
Giving it three words and expecting judgement
It matches the effort you put in. Four words in gives you a form letter back.
3
Asking it to decide, not to draft
It carries none of the consequences, so it will agree with whatever you seem to want.
4
Redoing the same prompt every week
The weekly job is the one worth saving. Store it once and refill the facts.
5
Not saying "do not add anything"
Without that line it will fill every gap it finds, and the gaps are invisible to you.

What good use looks like

Same job, less time

A three-hour formatting job becomes ten minutes of checking. The judgement stays with you.

Same quality, less strain

English you are not fully confident in stops being the reason a good point lands badly.

Better on the second try

You keep the prompt that worked, so next week starts where this week finished.

The capability rule

It is excellent at language and useless at knowing. Match your task to that.

Every good use and every bad use of these tools comes back to this one line.

  • Language work — give it to the tool.
  • Knowledge work — keep it on your side.
  • Judgement work — that is your job, always.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Capability
Something the tool can reliably do. Here it means language work, not knowledge work.
Prompt
Everything you type in: the request plus the facts you supply.
Output
What comes back. Your draft, which you are responsible for checking.
Hallucination
A confident, invented answer. Usually a name, a number, a date or a policy.
Context
The background you give so the tool knows who the text is for and why.
Model
The trained system behind the app you are typing into.
