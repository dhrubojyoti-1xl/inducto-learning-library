AI Fundamentals

What AI actually is, in plain language — and what it changes on your desk on Monday morning.

Why this matters

Ramesh loses two hours every Friday

Ramesh runs dispatch at the Bhiwandi warehouse. Every Friday he writes delay notes for the shipments that missed their date. He already knows every order number, every reason, every new date. What takes him two hours is finding the words.

Two hours a week. Around 100 hours a year. Spent on typing, not on dispatch.
What changes

You hand over the facts. A clean draft comes back in a minute. Your time goes into checking it, not typing it.

What AI actually is

The one idea behind every AI tool

It predicts the next word

An AI assistant does not look up an answer. It reads your words and works out what should come next, one small piece at a time, thousands of times a second.

1

You type

Your request goes in as plain text.

2

It splits your text

Words become small pieces called tokens.

3

It predicts

It picks the most likely next piece, then the next, then the next.

4

You get an answer

Sentences appear that fit the shape of what you asked for.

Terms on this page: Token, Model

It knows nothing about our company

Everything it can say came from text it read before a fixed cut-off date. None of your company's files were in that text.

Public text it was trained on

Books, websites, manuals, forums — up to a fixed date.

What it can discuss confidently

General knowledge, language, structure, formatting.

What it knows about our company

Nothing at all, unless you type it in yourself.

This is why it can write a perfectly worded dispatch note and still invent the client's name. It is filling a gap in a sentence, not remembering a fact.

Three words you will hear daily

Model

The trained system that produces the answer. ChatGPT, Copilot and Gemini are apps built around a model.

Prompt

Everything you type in. Your request, plus any facts you decide to give it.

Output

What comes back. Always a draft you are responsible for. Never a source you can quote.

Terms on this page: Prompt, Output

Search or assistant?

Two different tools, two different jobs

Search finds pages. AI writes text.

Search engine

Google, Bing, the company intranet

  • Returns links to pages that other people wrote
  • Right choice when you need a source you can quote
  • Everyone who searches the same words sees the same list
  • Will not rewrite your email or shorten your report
AI assistant

Copilot, ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude

  • Returns new text written for your exact request
  • Right choice when you need a draft, summary or rewrite
  • Gives a slightly different answer every time you ask
  • Cannot promise that any fact inside it is correct

Which one do I open?

Do I need a page someone else wrote, or new text of my own?
New text

Open the AI assistant

A reply to an unhappy client, a summary of a 12-page site report, a first draft of an SOP. You already have the facts and only need the wording.

A page

Open search

A GST circular, a supplier's spec sheet, a government notification, a news report. You need the actual page, because you will have to show it to someone.

Your first two minutes

Do it now, with the exact words

Do this now: your first 90 seconds

  1. Open your approved AI assistant. On most company laptops this is Microsoft Copilot, in Edge or inside Teams.
  2. Click into the message box at the bottom of the screen.
  3. Select the whole prompt on the right, copy it, paste it in, and press Enter.
  4. Read what comes back. Notice that it used every fact you gave it, and invented nothing.
Copy this prompt
Write a short, polite email to a customer whose delivery is late. Facts: order 4471, promised 12 March, now expected 15 March, delayed by a transport strike. Tone: apologetic but calm. Under 120 words. End with "Regards, Dispatch Team".
This works exactly as written. Swap in your own order number and dates afterwards.

The same job, asked two ways

Vague request
  • You type: write email about late delivery
  • You get back a six-paragraph letter with no order number, no dates, and an apology for "any inconvenience caused".
  • You now spend ten minutes repairing it. You could have written it yourself in eight.
Specific request
  • You type the prompt from the last slide: order number, both dates, the reason, the tone, the word limit.
  • You get back a 110-word email with the correct dates, one clear apology and a firm new delivery date.
  • You spend 60 seconds checking the dates against the tracking sheet, then send it.

The difference was not the AI tool. It was the four facts you took ten seconds to type.

Turn rough notes into a report

This is the most useful thing most people do with AI all week. You already made the notes. You just need them in sentences.

Copy this prompt
Turn these site-visit notes into a 180-word report for my manager. Keep every fact. Do not add anything I have not written. Notes: visited Chakan plant 4 March; two of six machines idle; operator says spare part on order since 20 Feb; production down about 30 percent; supervisor asking for a follow-up visit next week.
Paste it as-is to see the shape, then replace the notes with your own.
What comes back
  • A 180-word report with a one-line summary at the top, the five facts in order, and a closing line about the follow-up visit.
  • Every number in it — six machines, 20 February, 30 per cent — came from your notes, because you told it to add nothing.
  • Your job now is 60 seconds of checking, not 30 minutes of writing.

When it is 80% right, correct it

Copy this correction prompt
Keep the structure and the tone exactly as they are. Change three things: the delivery date is 15 March, not 12 March; delete the final paragraph; make the apology one sentence instead of two.
Say what to keep, then say what to change. In that order.
Why this works
  • Starting again gives you a different answer with the same gaps, because the gaps came from your first prompt.
  • Naming what to keep stops it rewriting the good 80%.
  • Three specific changes work far better than "make it better", which it cannot act on.
Where it helps, where it breaks

Pick the right task

Three things it is good at

Turning facts into text

Emails, summaries, SOP drafts, meeting notes. You supply the facts, it supplies the sentences.

Reshaping what you give it

A long report into five bullets. A Hindi note into English. A messy list into a clean table.

Getting you unstuck

Ten subject lines to choose from. Questions to ask a supplier. A structure for a proposal you are staring at blankly.

Where it breaks

It can be completely wrong and still sound completely sure.

There is no warning sign, no change of tone, no "I think". Every answer arrives with the same confidence.

  • Numbers you did not give it — prices, GST rates, stock levels, distances, penalties
  • Anything after its cut-off date — this month's circular, yesterday's rate change
  • Our own facts — client names, internal policies, system names, who approves what
  • Legal, tax or medical points you would act on without a human checking them

Terms on this page: Hallucination

Should I use AI for this task?

Do I already know the facts, and can I check the answer?
Yes

Go ahead

You give the facts, it gives the wording, you check it before it leaves your desk. This covers most of the writing in an ordinary office week.

No

Get the facts first

If you cannot check the answer, you cannot use it. Take the real number from the system, the circular or your manager, and then come back to the assistant.

Using it safely

The habits that keep you out of trouble

Four habits that keep you safe

Four checks before you press send

Five mistakes people really make

1
Pasting a full customer contract in "just to summarise it"
Client data leaves the company. Reportable under India's DPDP Act and UAE data protection law.
2
Asking for a live figure — today's GST rate, current stock
It answers from memory. A confidently wrong number lands in the monthly MIS.
3
Sending the first draft without reading it
Invented dates and order numbers reach the client with your name at the bottom.
4
Typing four words, getting a vague answer, giving up
You decide "AI is useless". The prompt was the problem, not the tool.
5
Using a personal ChatGPT login for company work
No record, no control, and no way to delete the data if we are ever asked to.

Recap

AI Fundamentals on one screen

It predicts, it does not look up

Every answer is built piece by piece from patterns in text, not fetched from a database of facts.

Search finds pages, AI writes text

Need something you can quote? Search. Need a draft, summary or rewrite? Assistant.

You bring the facts

It has none of our order numbers, clients, policies or systems until you type them in.

Check every number and name

A confident tone is not evidence. Verify before anything leaves your desk.

Facts, never files

Type the order number. Never paste the contract, the salary sheet or the client list.

Correct it, don't restart it

Say what to keep and what to change. The second answer is usually the one you send.

You supply the facts and the final check. The assistant supplies the sentences in between.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Token
A small piece of text, roughly three quarters of a word. AI tools read and write in tokens, not in letters.
Model
The trained system that produces the answer. ChatGPT, Copilot and Gemini are apps built around a model.
Prompt
Everything you type in: your request plus any facts you choose to supply.
Output
What comes back. Always a draft you are responsible for, never a source you can quote.
Hallucination
A confident answer that is simply made up. Usually a name, a number or a date.
Training data
The public text the model learned from, up to a fixed cut-off date. Our files were never in it.
