Critical Thinking

Telling evidence from assertion — including in your own reasoning, which is the harder half.

Why this matters

Nisha's number is repeated for a year

Nisha builds the monthly pack in Hyderabad. Someone once said returns run at about eight per cent. It went into a slide. It has been repeated in every pack since. Nobody has ever calculated it.

A year of decisions resting on a number nobody checked.
What changes

Three questions, asked out loud, before a claim gets repeated.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Tell a fact from an assertion in somebody else's argument
  • 2Ask where a number came from without sounding obstructive
  • 3Catch the two biases that affect your own work most
  • 4Test whether a comparison is actually comparing like with like
  • 5Disagree with a conclusion without attacking the person
Fact or assertion

The three questions

Three questions, every claim

Most workplace claims are true, unchecked, or true once and never re-examined. These three separate them.

How a number becomes a fact

1

Somebody estimates

"Returns are about eight per cent, I think."

2

It gets written down

The hedge disappears in the slide.

3

It gets repeated

Now three documents say eight per cent.

4

It becomes the figure

And decisions are made on it.

Where numbers come from

Asking without friction

Asking without friction

Copy these three questions
"Out of interest, where does the eight per cent come from — is that from the system?" / "Do we know roughly when that was last calculated?" / "Is that all products, or the main line?"
Curious, not challenging. Almost nobody takes offence at these.
Why this works
  • "Out of interest" removes any accusation from the question.
  • Asking when it was calculated is less confrontational than asking if it is right.
  • The scope question often reveals the real problem.

Three kinds of number

Traceable

You can open the source

  • Comes from a system report you can rerun
  • Has a date and a defined scope
  • Somebody can tell you how it is calculated
  • Safe to put in front of a customer
Folklore

Nobody can point at it

  • "We have always said about eight per cent"
  • No date, no scope, no method
  • Appears in three documents, all copying each other
  • Repeated confidently by everyone
Your own two biases

The ones that matter at work

The two that matter at work

Confirmation

You notice the data that fits your view and genuinely do not register the rest.

Recency

Last week's complaint feels like a trend. Six quiet months do not feel like anything.

The counter

Ask what evidence would change your mind, before you go looking at any evidence.

Terms on this page: Confirmation bias

Deciding in advance

Looking for support
  • You think the new supplier is causing the rejects.
  • You pull the reject data and find three from their batches.
  • You stop there, because you found what you expected.
Deciding the test first
  • "If their batches reject at the same rate as the old supplier, I am wrong."
  • You pull both rates. Theirs is 2.1, the old one 1.9.
  • That is not the cause. You keep looking, a week earlier than you would have.

Deciding what would change your mind before you look is the only reliable protection against finding what you expected.

Bad comparisons

Like with like

Like with like

1
Comparing a full month with a part month
November against three weeks of December. The drop is the calendar, not the business.
2
Comparing before and after a definition change
"Rejects" started including rework in April. The rise is a counting change.
3
Comparing a total with a rate
More rejects with far more volume can be a lower rejection rate.
4
Comparing across a changed scope
Two sites last year, four this year. Everything looks like growth.

The four checks

Do this now

Test a real claim

Do this now: test one claim

  1. Find a number that appears in your regular reporting.
  2. Ask where it came from and when it was last calculated.
  3. Check whether the comparison periods are actually comparable.
  4. If nobody can answer, put a date and a source on it yourself.
Copy this prompt
Here is a claim and the reasoning behind it. List the assumptions it depends on, marking each as STATED or UNSTATED. Then list what evidence would show the claim is wrong. Do not tell me whether you think it is true.
"Do not tell me whether you think it is true" is what makes this useful.

Disagreeing well

Copy this wording
"I might be missing something — can I check one thing? The comparison is November against December, but December only has three working weeks in it. Does that account for most of the drop, or is there something else?"
Question the reasoning, never the person.
Why this works
  • "I might be missing something" costs nothing and lowers the temperature.
  • It names one specific thing rather than doubting the whole analysis.
  • It ends with a genuine question, so there is an easy way out.

Four habits worth having

Five mistakes people really make

1
Repeating a number because it is in a previous deck
Three documents agreeing usually means one source copied twice.
2
Treating a hedge as a fact
"About eight per cent, I think" becomes "8%" in the next slide.
3
Looking for evidence after forming the view
You will find it. That is what confirmation bias reliably does.
4
Comparing periods of different lengths
The most common bad comparison in any monthly pack anywhere.
5
Challenging the person instead of the reasoning
The argument stops being about the number within one sentence.

When to stop questioning

Would a better number change what we do?
Yes

Keep asking

If the decision turns on it, the number deserves a source and a date. Spend the hour — it is much cheaper than the wrong decision.

No

Let it go

If we would do the same thing whether it is six or ten per cent, precision is not worth a meeting. Note it as approximate and move on.

The critical thinking rule

Ask where it came from, when it was true, and what would change your mind.

The third question is the one people skip, and the only one that works on your own reasoning.

  • Where did it come from?
  • When was it true?
  • What would change my mind?

Glossary

Words used in this module

Assertion
A claim stated without evidence. Often true, and not yet demonstrated.
Confirmation bias
Noticing evidence that fits your view and not registering the rest.
Recency bias
Treating the most recent event as a trend, and quiet periods as nothing.
Like-for-like
A comparison where period, definition and scope are all the same.
Folklore
A number everybody repeats that nobody can trace to a source.
Falsification
Deciding in advance what evidence would show you are wrong.
