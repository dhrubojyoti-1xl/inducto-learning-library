Evaluating AI Responses

A thirty-second way to decide whether to send it, fix it or throw it away.

Why this matters

Neha sends a draft she half-read

Neha writes training notes for a Noida team. The AI draft reads well, so she skims it and sends it. Two paragraphs in, it describes a sign-off step the company does not have. Fourteen people follow it for a fortnight.

A fortnight of a process nobody actually approved.
What changes

Four questions, thirty seconds, before anything is sent.

The four questions

Thirty seconds, every time

Four questions, thirty seconds

Ask these in order. Most answers fail on question two, and you stop there.

Why fluency fools people

1

It reads well

Clean sentences, confident tone, no hedging.

2

You relax

Good writing signals care, in every other context.

3

You skim

You check the wording rather than the content.

4

The middle slips through

Where the invented line usually is.

Send, fix or bin

Choosing correctly

Send, fix or throw away

Is the structure right and only the details wrong?
Yes

Fix it

Name what to keep, name what to change, supply the correct fact. One line of correction beats any amount of rewriting.

No

Throw it away

If the shape is wrong, the prompt was wrong. Correcting it round by round costs more than one better prompt would.

The three-way test in practice

Fix it

Structure right, details wrong

  • Correct shape, one wrong date
  • Right tone, forty words too long
  • Good order, one invented line
  • Almost there, missing the closing ask
Start again

It answered a different question

  • Written for the wrong reader entirely
  • General advice where you wanted specifics
  • Ignored the constraint you cared most about
  • Three rounds in and still drifting
Writing the correction

Keep what worked

Keep, change, supply

Copy this correction pattern
Keep the structure, the tone and the first two paragraphs exactly as they are. Change two things: the review step in paragraph three does not exist, so remove it; and the deadline is 15 March, not 12 March. Reissue the whole thing.
Keep, change, supply — in that order, every time.
Why this works
  • Naming what to keep stops it rewriting the good part.
  • Two specific changes work far better than "fix this".
  • Supplying the right date means nothing gets invented.

Corrections that do not work

1
"Make it better"
There is no scale for better. You get a different draft with a different set of problems.
2
"That is wrong, try again"
It does not know which part. Usually it rewrites the part that was fine.
3
"Shorter"
It picks an amount. You will be back in a minute asking again. Give a number.
4
Listing eight changes at once
The first three land, the rest drift. Two or three per round is the reliable limit.
Fluent but empty

The answer that dodges

The answer that dodges

Some answers are perfectly written and contain nothing. They are the easiest to miss.

Fluent and empty
  • You asked: "Should we accept a 45-day payment term?"
  • You got: three balanced paragraphs on the general considerations around payment terms.
  • Nothing in it referred to your cash position or your customer.
Fluent and useful
  • You asked again with your facts and a forced answer.
  • "Given the figures below, give one recommendation and two reasons. No balanced overview."
  • You got a position you could argue with, which is what you needed.

Balance is what it produces when it has nothing specific to say. Ask for a position and it stops.

Three signs of an empty answer

It lists considerations

"There are several factors to weigh." You already knew that. You wanted the weighing.

It restates your question

A paragraph explaining what you asked is a paragraph with no answer in it.

It hedges both ways

"It depends on your circumstances" is true, useless and infinitely repeatable.

Do this now

Score a real answer

Do this now: score one answer

  1. Open the last AI answer you actually used at work.
  2. Run the four questions on it: traceable, complete, usable, safe.
  3. Mark any sentence you could not trace to your own input.
  4. Send yourself the corrected version and notice how long it took.
Copy this prompt
Score your own last answer against four questions and reply in four short lines. One: which facts came from my message and which from you. Two: which part of my question you did not answer. Three: what you would need from me to improve it. Four: nothing else.
A useful second pass, not a substitute for reading it yourself.

Comparing two answers fast

Copy this comparison prompt
Below are two versions of the same message. List only the differences in substance — facts, commitments, dates and numbers. Ignore differences in wording and style completely. Use one line per difference.
Saves re-reading two long drafts line by line.
What comes back
  • A short list of the real differences, usually three or four.
  • No commentary about which version reads better.
  • You choose on substance in under a minute.

Four habits before you send

The round-four rule

If you are on the fourth correction, the prompt is the problem, not the answer.

Rewriting the prompt takes two minutes. Round five takes longer and usually fails too.

  • Round one to two — normal.
  • Round three — check your prompt.
  • Round four — start again.

Five mistakes people really make

1
Judging an answer by how well it reads
Fluency is constant. It tells you nothing about whether the content is right.
2
Checking the first and last paragraphs
The invented line is almost always in the middle, where attention drops.
3
Accepting a balanced overview as an answer
Balance is the default when it has nothing specific. Ask for a position.
4
Correcting the same draft five times
Three rounds of drift means the prompt was wrong. Fix that instead.
5
Asking the tool whether its answer is good
It will say yes, at length, in the same confident voice.

The evaluation rule

Judge it on what it says, never on how well it says it.

Every AI answer is well written. That is the one signal you can safely ignore.

  • Traceable and complete — send it.
  • Right shape, wrong details — fix it.
  • Wrong question answered — bin it.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Evaluation
Deciding whether an answer can be sent, needs fixing, or should be thrown away.
Traceable
Every fact in the answer can be pointed back to something you supplied.
Correction
A follow-up naming what to keep, what to change, and the correct value.
Hedging
Answering both ways at once. Usually a sign it has nothing specific to say.
Prompt
Everything you type in: context plus instructions plus your facts.
Output
What comes back. Your draft, which you are responsible for checking.
