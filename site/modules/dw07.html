Documentation with AI

SOPs, process notes and handovers somebody can follow without ringing you to ask what you meant.

Why this matters

Fatima is the only one who knows

Fatima handles vendor onboarding for a Dubai office. She has done it for four years and it lives entirely in her head. When she took two weeks' leave, three onboardings stalled and her phone rang eleven times.

Eleven calls on annual leave, and three vendors kept waiting.
What changes

Two hours of talking it through, and a process anyone can follow.

The curse of knowing

Why experts write bad steps

Experts write the worst steps

The steps you leave out are exactly the ones you stopped noticing years ago.

1

You know it cold

Four years of doing it without thinking.

2

The obvious vanishes

Which system, which tab, whose approval.

3

You write six steps

Where a new joiner needs fourteen.

4

They ring you

At every gap you could not see.

What gets left out

You wrote

"Raise the vendor record"

  • One line that feels complete
  • Obvious to anyone who has done it
  • Six words covering four minutes of work
  • No system, no field, no approval named
They need

Four separate steps

  • Which system, and how to reach it
  • Which fields are mandatory, and what goes in them
  • Who approves it, and how long they take
  • What to do when the code already exists
Talk it, then shape it

Dictate, do not compose

Talk it through, do not compose

Explaining it out loud to an imaginary new joiner produces far better raw material than writing does.

Copy this shaping prompt
Below is me explaining a process in my own words, in no particular order. Turn it into numbered steps. Each step must be one action a person can complete. Use only what I said. Where I have skipped something a beginner would need, write [GAP: what is missing] instead of guessing.
Type or dictate however you like. Order does not matter.
Why this works
  • [GAP] finds the steps you stopped noticing years ago.
  • "One action per step" stops four jobs hiding in one line.
  • You can ramble. Reordering is exactly what it is good at.

What a good step looks like

Testing a procedure

The new-joiner test

The new-joiner test

Copy this test prompt
Read these steps as somebody who joined the company yesterday and has never seen this system. List every point where you would have to stop and ask a question. Do not rewrite the steps and do not answer your own questions.
The questions are the value. Ignore any urge to have it fix them.
What comes back
  • Six to ten questions, most of which you would never have predicted.
  • Usually one about an approval you forgot anyone needed.
  • You answer them in the document, and the phone stops ringing.

Then test it on a person

Untested SOP
  • Written by the expert, reviewed by the expert.
  • Reads perfectly to anyone who already knows the process.
  • First new joiner rings on step three.
Tested SOP
  • Given to somebody who has never done it, with no help.
  • You watch and write down every place they hesitate.
  • Ten minutes of watching removes a year of phone calls.

The AI test finds the obvious gaps. A real person finds the ones neither of you expected.

Keeping it current

Documents that rot

Documents rot quietly

Date it

"Last checked March 2026" tells a reader whether to trust it or ring you.

Own it

One named role, not a team. Documents with no owner are never updated.

Fix on use

The moment somebody hits a wrong step, correct the document, not just the person.

The update prompt

Copy this update prompt
Here is an existing procedure and a description of what has changed. Update only the steps affected by the change. Leave every other step exactly as written, including the wording. List at the end which step numbers you changed.
"List which steps you changed" makes review take seconds.
Why this works
  • Untouched steps stay untouched, so nothing drifts.
  • The change list is what you review, not the whole document.
  • Updating becomes a two-minute job, so it actually happens.
Do this now

Document one real process

Do this now: document one

  1. Pick the process only you know how to do.
  2. Explain it out loud into a document for ten minutes.
  3. Run the shaping prompt, then the new-joiner test prompt.
  4. Answer every [GAP] and every question it raises.
Copy this prompt
Turn my explanation below into numbered steps. One action per step, starting with a verb, naming the system and screen. Use only what I said. Write [GAP: what is missing] wherever a beginner would need something I have not mentioned.
Two prompts and forty minutes replaces a year of interruptions.

What not to put in a procedure

An SOP gets shared widely and lives for years. Write it accordingly.

Name roles and systems, never people and credentials.

  • Passwords, PINs or shared login details
  • A named individual where a role would do
  • Real customer records used as the worked example
  • Internal system addresses posted outside the company

Five mistakes people really make

1
Writing steps from memory, in order
You write what you remember doing, not what a beginner has to do.
2
Putting four actions in one step
"Raise and approve the record and notify the vendor" is three steps and one guess.
3
Naming a person instead of a role
The document breaks the day they change job, and nobody notices for months.
4
Never testing it on a real beginner
The expert review confirms only that experts can follow it.
5
Leaving it undated
A reader cannot tell whether it is current, so they ring you to check anyway.

How much detail is right?

Could somebody do this wrong and not realise?
Yes

Add the check

Name what success looks like at that step. "The status turns green" or "you receive a confirmation email within ten minutes".

No

Keep it short

If a mistake is immediately obvious, one line is enough. Over-documenting the obvious is how SOPs become unreadable.

The handover document

The documentation rule

Write it for the person who has never done it, not for the person who already can.

Every phone call you get is a step you left out because it was obvious to you.

  • Talk it, then shape it.
  • Answer every [GAP].
  • Test it on a beginner.

Glossary

Words used in this module

SOP
Standard Operating Procedure. A written process somebody can follow without asking questions.
[GAP]
A marker the tool writes where a beginner would need information you did not supply.
Handover
A document covering what you do, who to call, and what usually goes wrong while you are away.
Curse of knowledge
The habit of leaving out steps that have become invisible to you through practice.
Prompt
Everything you type in: your explanation and the constraints on shaping it.
Output
What comes back. Your draft, which you are responsible for checking.
