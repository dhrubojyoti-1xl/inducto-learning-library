Password Security

Why one reused password is the most likely way this company gets broken into.

Why this matters

Sanjay used the same password twice

Sanjay runs stores at a Nashik unit. Six years ago he used one password for a shopping site. He uses the same one at work. The shopping site was breached in 2021. Nobody told him, and the password still works here.

An attacker does not need to guess. They already have it.
What changes

Different passwords everywhere, remembered by a manager, not by you.

How accounts get taken

Not by guessing

Nobody sits and guesses

The film version is somebody typing guesses. The real version is a list of passwords that already leaked.

1

A website is breached

Somewhere you signed up, years ago, possibly forgotten.

2

The list is sold

Email addresses and passwords, millions at a time.

3

Software tries them

Automatically, against every well-known service.

4

One of them works

Because it was reused. That is the whole attack.

What actually gets used

What people worry about

Guessing your password

  • Someone trying combinations by hand
  • Needing to know your dog's name
  • Symbols and capitals stopping them
  • Rare, slow and mostly fictional
What actually happens

Trying passwords that leaked

  • Your real password, from another site
  • Tried automatically against hundreds of services
  • Symbols and capitals do not help at all
  • Common, fast and completely routine
Reuse is the real risk

One breach, every account

One breach, every account

One password you reuse

Chosen once, years ago, still in use.

Every site you used it on

Shopping, forums, an old email account.

Including work

The weakest of those sites decides our security.

Your work account is only as safe as the least careful website you ever signed up to. That is what reuse actually means.

Terms on this page: Credential stuffing

Small changes do not help

1
Adding a number at the end
Attack software tries the obvious variations automatically. It is the first thing it does.
2
Changing one letter to a symbol
Replacing a with @ has been in every cracking list for twenty years.
3
Using the same base word everywhere
Once one is known, the pattern is known, and the rest follow in seconds.
4
Changing it only when forced to
A password that leaked in 2021 stays valid until the day you change it.
Building a strong one

Length beats symbols

Length beats symbols

Three or four unrelated words are harder to break and far easier to type than eight random characters.

Weak, medium, strong

Feels strong, is not
  • Nashik@2024
  • Eleven characters, a capital, a symbol and a number.
  • It is a place name and a year. Both are in every list.
Looks simple, is strong
  • copper-lantern-quiet-mango
  • Twenty-six characters, four unrelated words, easy to type.
  • Nothing about you appears in it and nothing is guessable.

Complexity rules made passwords hard for humans and barely harder for software. Length is what works.

Letting software remember

Password managers

Stop trying to remember

One password to learn

You memorise one long phrase. The manager holds every other password for you.

Different everywhere

It generates a unique password per site, so a breach anywhere affects nothing else.

It spots fakes

It will not fill your password into a lookalike site, because the address does not match.

Where passwords must never live

If it is written somewhere a colleague can read it, it is not a password any more.

  • A note under the keyboard or in a desk drawer
  • A spreadsheet called passwords, shared or not
  • A WhatsApp message to yourself or a colleague
  • An AI chat window, ever, for any reason
Do this now

Check and change one

Do this now: check and change

  1. Open the breach-check site listed in your toolkit.
  2. Type your work email address. It tells you which breaches included it.
  3. If your work password matches any personal one, change it today.
  4. Set the new one as four unrelated words, generated by your password manager's own generator, not typed by you and never asked of an AI tool.

If a password is exposed

Five mistakes people really make

1
Using a work password on a personal site
That site's security is now our security, and we have no say in it.
2
Sharing a login "just for today"
Shared logins remove any record of who did what, permanently.
3
Keeping a password because it is strong
Strength is irrelevant once it has leaked. Reuse is the problem, not weakness.
4
Storing passwords in the browser on a shared machine
Anyone who sits at that desk has every account you have saved.
5
Assuming an old account does not matter
The forgotten account is usually the one that leaked and gave up the password.

Sharing an account is never fine

Does somebody else need access to this system?
Yes

Get them an account

Their own login, their own password. It takes a day and it means every action has a name against it if anything is ever questioned.

Sharing yours

Never do this

Anything they do appears as you. If money moves or data leaves, the record says you did it, and you cannot prove otherwise.

Four habits worth having

The password rule

Unique matters far more than complicated.

A simple password used once is safer than a complex one used twice.

  • Unique — the only thing that stops reuse attacks.
  • Long — sixteen characters or more.
  • Stored — by software, not by memory.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Credential stuffing
Trying leaked email and password pairs automatically against many services at once.
Breach
A site losing its user list, usually including email addresses and passwords.
Passphrase
A password made of several unrelated words. Long, memorable and hard to break.
Password manager
Software that stores a unique password for every account, behind one phrase you learn.
Shared account
One login used by several people. It destroys any record of who did what.
Multi-factor
A second proof of identity beyond the password. Covered in SEC-03.
