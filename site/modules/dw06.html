Presentations with AI

Get the argument straight before you open PowerPoint. The slides are the easy part.

Why this matters

Tanvi builds 34 slides nobody needs

Tanvi prepares a quarterly review for a Mumbai group office. She opens PowerPoint first and builds 34 slides over two evenings. In the meeting she is stopped on slide four and asked the one question the deck never answers.

Two evenings, and the question still unanswered.
What changes

Settle the argument in ten lines. Then build only what supports it.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Write the argument before you open any slide software
  • 2Turn a rough argument into a slide-by-slide outline
  • 3Cut a deck to the slides that carry the point
  • 4Predict the three questions you will actually be asked
  • 5Write speaker notes you can genuinely say out loud
Argument before slides

The ten-line test

Write the argument first

If you cannot say it in ten lines, thirty slides will not rescue it.

1

The point

One sentence. What you want them to agree with.

2

The reasons

Three, no more. Each one defensible.

3

The evidence

One figure or example per reason.

4

The ask

What you want them to decide or do.

The ten-line test

Copy this argument prompt
Below are my rough notes for a presentation. Turn them into an argument in exactly this shape: THE POINT, one sentence. THREE REASONS, one line each. EVIDENCE, one line under each reason. THE ASK, one sentence. Use only my notes. Write [WEAK] beside any reason I have not evidenced.
Ten lines. If they do not convince you, the deck will not convince anyone.
Why this works
  • [WEAK] shows you which reason will get you stopped.
  • Three reasons is a limit, and limits force choices.
  • You find the hole at your desk, not in the room.
Outline, then build

One slide, one point

One slide, one point

Copy this prompt
Turn this argument into a slide outline. One slide per point, maximum eight slides. For each, give me a title that states the point as a full sentence, and one line saying what the visual should show. Do not write slide content and do not suggest stock images.
A title that states the point is worth more than any layout.
What comes back
  • Eight slide titles that each say something, rather than naming a topic.
  • One line per slide telling you what to actually draw or chart.
  • A deck you can build in an hour because every decision is made.

Titles that say something

Topic titles
  • "Q3 Performance"
  • "Challenges"
  • The reader has to work out your point from the chart underneath.
Sentence titles
  • "Dispatch volumes held, but rejections doubled"
  • "Two of six machines are idle waiting for one part"
  • Someone reading only the titles understands the whole argument.

If your titles alone tell the story, the deck works even when it is forwarded without you.

Cutting the deck

Half the slides, same message

Half the slides, same message

The appendix is your friend

Main deck

Only what carries the argument. Usually six to eight slides for a routine review.

Appendix

Everything you might be asked for. Nobody penalises you for having it ready.

Nothing deleted

Cutting is reordering, not throwing away. That makes it much easier to do.

Preparing for questions

Before you are asked

Know the questions first

Being stopped on slide four is normal. Being unprepared for it is a choice.

Copy this question prompt
Here is my argument and the evidence behind it. List the five questions a sceptical senior manager would ask, hardest first. For each, say in one line what evidence would answer it. Do not answer the questions and do not soften them.
"Hardest first" and "do not soften them" are what make this useful.
Why this works
  • You find your weakest reason before the room does.
  • "What evidence would answer it" tells you what to go and get.
  • Three of the five usually get asked, almost word for word.

Speaker notes you can say

Copy this notes prompt
For each slide title below, write speaker notes as spoken sentences, maximum 40 words per slide. Write them the way somebody would actually say them out loud, not the way they would be written. No bullet points and no formal phrasing.
Notes you can read aloud beat notes you have to translate.
What comes back
  • Forty spoken words per slide, in sentences you could say without editing.
  • No "it should be noted that", because you asked for spoken English.
  • Something you can actually glance at while talking.
Do this now

Outline a real deck

Do this now: outline a deck

  1. Take the next presentation you owe someone.
  2. Write your rough notes in any order, for five minutes.
  3. Run the ten-line argument prompt on them.
  4. Fix anything marked [WEAK] before you open PowerPoint.
Copy this prompt
Turn my notes into an argument: THE POINT (one sentence), THREE REASONS (one line each), EVIDENCE (one line each), THE ASK (one sentence). Use only my notes, add no figures, and mark any unevidenced reason [WEAK].
Ten minutes here saves two evenings of building.

What not to paste in

A review deck usually contains the most commercially sensitive material you handle.

Describe the shape of the numbers instead of pasting them.

  • Customer names against revenue or margin
  • Unpublished results, forecasts or board figures
  • Employee names against performance
  • Anything from a contract or a tender response

Five mistakes people really make

1
Opening PowerPoint first
You spend the evening on layout and discover the argument has a hole in the meeting.
2
Asking AI to "make a presentation"
You get thirty generic slides with headings and no position in any of them.
3
Topic titles instead of sentences
"Q3 Performance" makes the reader do the work. Forwarded without you, it says nothing.
4
Using generated images on customer slides
Almost-words and odd details read as carelessness about everything else.
5
Skipping the question rehearsal
The hardest question is entirely predictable, and you had ten minutes to predict it.

Should AI build the slides?

Does the deck carry a decision or a number?
Yes

You build it

Board papers, customer proposals, budget reviews. Use AI for the argument, the outline and the questions. Build the slides yourself.

No

Draft it freely

Team updates, training material, internal explainers. Let it draft structure and notes, then edit hard.

What good preparation looks like

Ten lines of argument

Written and tested before anything is designed.

Eight slide titles

Each a full sentence that states a point.

Five questions rehearsed

Hardest first, with the evidence located.

Thirty minutes of this beats two evenings of building, and it is the part that decides whether the meeting goes well.

The presentation rule

If the titles alone do not tell the story, the deck is not finished.

Slides get forwarded without you far more often than you present them.

  • Argument first — always.
  • One slide, one point.
  • Titles that say something.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Argument
The point you want agreed, the reasons for it, and what you are asking for.
Sentence title
A slide heading that states the point rather than naming the topic.
Appendix
Slides kept after the end, ready if asked, invisible if not.
Speaker notes
What you will actually say, written as spoken sentences.
[WEAK]
A marker showing a reason you have asserted without evidence.
Prompt
Everything you type in: your notes, the structure and the constraints.
