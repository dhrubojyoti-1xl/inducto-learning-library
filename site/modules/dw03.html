Report Preparation with AI

Turning what you already know into a report somebody actually reads to the end.

Why this matters

Ravi writes reports nobody finishes

Ravi is a site engineer covering three plants near Pune. His visit reports are thorough and eleven pages long. His manager reads the first page and rings him for the rest, every single time.

Six hours of writing, and the same phone call anyway.
What changes

The answer first, in one paragraph. The detail underneath it.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Put the answer in the first paragraph, every time
  • 2Turn rough site notes into a readable report in ten minutes
  • 3Keep one report structure your team recognises on sight
  • 4Cut a report in half without losing anything that matters
  • 5Make sure every number in it traces to something real
Answer first

Why page eleven is too late

Page eleven is too late

Most work reports are written in the order the work happened. Nobody reads in that order.

Written in order

How the work happened

  • Background and scope of the visit
  • What was inspected, area by area
  • Observations in the sequence found
  • Conclusion and recommendation, page eleven
Answer first

How it gets read

  • The recommendation, in one paragraph
  • The three findings behind it
  • What happens next, and who does it
  • Everything else, for whoever wants it

The one paragraph that matters

Copy this opening-paragraph prompt
Write the opening paragraph of a report, under 80 words. It must state the conclusion, the single most important reason for it, and what I am asking the reader to do. Use only the facts below. Do not add background, and do not describe what the report contains.
If your reader stops after this paragraph, they still know what to do.
Why this works
  • "Do not describe what the report contains" kills the usual filler.
  • Conclusion, reason, ask — the three things a manager needs.
  • Eighty words forces a real decision about what matters.
The five-part shape

One structure, every report

One shape for every report

Why a fixed shape helps

Faster to write

The structure stops being a decision, so you spend your time on content instead.

Faster to read

Your manager learns where things live and stops hunting for the recommendation.

Comparable

Six site reports in the same shape can be read side by side. Six freeform ones cannot.

Notes into report

Ten minutes, grounded

Rough notes into a report

Copy this prompt
Turn these site notes into a report using this exact structure: WHAT I FOUND, WHY IT MATTERS, WHAT I RECOMMEND, WHAT I SAW. Use only my notes. Add no causes, no numbers and no recommendations I have not written. Where something is missing, write [CHECK]. Under 400 words.
Then paste your notes underneath, however rough they are.
What comes back
  • A four-part report in the shape your team already recognises.
  • [CHECK] wherever your notes were thin, instead of an invented cause.
  • Six hours of writing becomes twenty minutes of checking and editing.

The line that protects you

"Add no causes I have not written" is the most important line in a report prompt.

Reports are where invented explanations do the most damage, because people act on them.

  • It will suggest a cause if you let it.
  • The cause will sound reasonable.
  • Somebody will fix the wrong thing.
Cutting it in half

Without losing substance

Cut it without losing it

Copy this cutting prompt
Cut this report to half its length. Keep every number, date, name of a location and recommendation exactly as written. Remove background, repetition and anything that describes the report itself. Do not rewrite the conclusions.
Naming what to protect is what makes cutting safe.
Why this works
  • Without the protection list it cuts numbers first.
  • Background and self-description are usually a third of it.
  • The conclusions stay in your words, not its paraphrase.

What to cut, what to keep

Eleven pages
  • Two pages of background the reader already knows.
  • Every area inspected, including the ones with nothing to report.
  • The recommendation on page eleven, in a paragraph about next steps.
Four pages
  • Recommendation in the first eighty words.
  • Three findings that actually changed something.
  • Full evidence still there, at the back, for whoever wants it.

Nothing was lost. It was reordered so that stopping early still leaves the reader informed.

Do this now

Rewrite one real report

Do this now: rewrite one

  1. Open the last report you wrote that got a phone call back.
  2. Find your recommendation and move it to the top.
  3. Paste it in with the cutting prompt and a word limit.
  4. Compare the two. Send the short one next time.
Copy this prompt
Rewrite this report so the conclusion and recommendation come first, in under 80 words, followed by the three findings that support them, then everything else. Keep all numbers and dates exactly. Change no conclusions. Mark anything unclear as [CHECK].
The fastest improvement most people can make to their writing.

Before it leaves your desk

Five mistakes people really make

1
Letting it explain why something happened
It was not there. A plausible cause sends somebody to fix the wrong machine.
2
Writing in the order the work happened
Your reader is not retracing your day. They want the answer and then the proof.
3
Asking for "a professional report"
You get headings, filler and an executive summary that summarises nothing.
4
Cutting without protecting the numbers
Told simply to shorten, it drops figures before it drops adjectives.
5
Keeping the self-describing paragraph
"This report sets out the findings of..." is fifty words that inform nobody.

Reports you should not automate

Does this report make a judgement about a person?
Yes

Write it yourself

Performance, conduct, capability, incident reports involving individuals. Your judgement, in your words. Use AI to check clarity afterwards at most.

No

Draft it with AI

Site visits, monthly summaries, project updates, technical findings. You supply the observations and it supplies the structure and the sentences.

What a good report does

First paragraph

The reader knows what to do, and can stop here.

Next half page

They know why, and can defend it to someone else.

The rest

Evidence, available if anyone challenges it.

Almost nobody reads a report end to end. Writing for the reader who stops early is not dumbing down — it is the whole craft.

The report rule

You supply the findings. It supplies the structure. Nobody supplies the causes but you.

Findings and causes are different things, and only one of them can be observed.

  • Observations — yours.
  • Structure and wording — its job.
  • Causes — only what you established.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Executive summary
The opening paragraph carrying the conclusion, the reason and the ask.
Finding
Something you observed. Different from a cause, which is an explanation of it.
Cause
Why something happened. Only you can establish this, and only if you checked.
[CHECK]
A marker the tool writes where a fact is missing, instead of inventing one.
Prompt
Everything you type in: your notes, the structure and the constraints.
Output
What comes back. Your draft, which you are responsible for checking.
