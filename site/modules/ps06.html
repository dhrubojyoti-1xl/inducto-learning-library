Problem-Solving

Finding the actual cause before you spend a fortnight fixing the wrong thing.

Why this matters

Bhavesh fixes it three times

Bhavesh maintains packing machines in Rajkot. The sealer keeps jamming. He replaces the belt in March, the roller in May and the motor in July. It jams again in August. Nobody has asked why it started jamming in February.

Three repairs, three shutdowns, and the same fault.
What changes

Five questions before any money is spent.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Separate the symptom from the cause, deliberately
  • 2Use five questions to reach the real cause in ten minutes
  • 3Test a suspected cause before committing to a fix
  • 4Know when to stop analysing and just try something
  • 5Write up a problem so somebody else can help you
Symptom and cause

Fixing the wrong thing

Fixing what you can see

The symptom is what interrupts you. The cause is usually somewhere quieter, and further back.

The symptom

What you notice

  • The sealer jams
  • The report is late every month
  • The customer complains about damage
  • Fixing this brings it back next month
The cause

What actually changed

  • Film thickness changed with a new supplier
  • The source data arrives two days late
  • Pallets are being stacked one layer higher
  • Fixing this ends it permanently

The question nobody asks

1

It broke

Everyone focuses here, because it hurts.

2

When did it start?

The single most useful question there is.

3

What changed then?

Suppliers, people, settings, volumes, weather.

4

That is your candidate

Test it before spending anything.

Five whys

Ten minutes, no cost

Five whys, ten minutes

Copy this structure
The sealer jams. Why? — The film is not feeding evenly. Why? — The tension arm is slipping. Why? — The film roll is heavier than before. Why? — We changed supplier in February. Why? — The new film is 20 microns thicker.
Stop when the answer is something you can actually change.
Why this works
  • Each answer must be a fact, not a guess.
  • If you cannot answer a why, that is what to go and check.
  • Five is a guide. Sometimes three is enough.

Terms on this page: Root cause

Where the chain goes wrong

1
Answering with a guess
One guessed link and everything after it is fiction, confidently pursued.
2
Stopping at "human error"
Almost never the end. Why was the error possible, and why was it not caught?
3
Stopping at something you cannot change
"Because it is monsoon season" ends the chain without producing an action.
4
Only asking why once
The first answer is the symptom wearing a different coat.
Testing before spending

The cheap experiment

The cheap experiment first

Before you spend money or a fortnight, find the smallest test that would prove you wrong.

One day beats three repairs

Fixing without testing
  • Replace the belt. It jams again in six weeks.
  • Replace the roller. It jams again in eight weeks.
  • Three parts, three shutdowns, same fault in August.
One day of testing
  • Run the old film on one machine for a single shift.
  • No jams on that machine. Jams on the other three.
  • Cause confirmed for the cost of one day and no parts.

A test that could prove you wrong is worth more than three fixes that assumed you were right.

When to stop analysing

Analysis has a limit

Analysis has a limit

Is the fix cheaper than another day of investigation?
Yes

Just try it

If a change costs an hour and reverses easily, trying it is the cheapest experiment available. Analysis is not free either.

No

Keep digging

If the fix costs real money, a shutdown, or is hard to undo, another day of asking why is very well spent indeed.

Write it up so others can help

Copy this problem write-up
PROBLEM: what is happening, in one sentence. STARTED: the date it first appeared. CHANGED THEN: anything that changed around that date. RULED OUT: what we have already tested and eliminated. NEXT TEST: the cheapest thing that would prove us wrong.
Five lines. Anybody can now help you without a meeting.
Why it works
  • "Started" and "changed then" together do most of the diagnostic work.
  • "Ruled out" stops three colleagues suggesting the same thing.
  • "Next test" turns a complaint into a plan somebody can act on.
Do this now

Work through a real problem

Do this now: one real problem

  1. Pick something that keeps coming back.
  2. Write down exactly when it first appeared.
  3. List everything that changed in that fortnight.
  4. Design the cheapest test that would prove you wrong.
Copy this prompt
I have a recurring problem. Ask me one question at a time to work through five whys. Do not suggest causes yourself and do not skip ahead. If I answer with a guess rather than a fact, say so and ask how I could check it.
"Do not suggest causes yourself" is what keeps this honest.

Four habits worth having

Five mistakes people really make

1
Replacing the part that broke
The part broke for a reason. The new one will meet the same reason.
2
Stopping the chain at human error
The real question is why the error was possible and why nothing caught it.
3
Testing to confirm rather than to disprove
You will find confirmation for almost any theory if that is what you look for.
4
Not writing down what was ruled out
Three people test the same thing across two months and nobody knows.
5
Analysing something that costs an hour to just try
Analysis is not free. Cheap, reversible fixes are their own experiment.

Problems that are not technical

The late report

Started in April. What changed? The source data moved to a different team.

The repeated complaint

Started when a shift pattern changed. Nobody connected the two.

The rising rejection rate

Started with a new material grade, three weeks before anyone noticed.

The problem-solving rule

Ask when it started, find what changed then, and test that before you spend anything.

Most recurring problems at work have a start date and a change beside it.

  • When did it start?
  • What changed then?
  • What would prove me wrong?

Glossary

Words used in this module

Symptom
What you notice when something is wrong. Usually not the thing to fix.
Root cause
The change that started it. Fixing this stops the problem returning.
Five whys
Asking why repeatedly, with a fact at each step, until you reach something changeable.
Falsification
Designing a test that would show you were wrong. Worth more than confirmation.
Ruled out
Something already tested and eliminated. Record it so nobody repeats it.
Reversible
A change you can undo cheaply. Reversible fixes make good experiments.
