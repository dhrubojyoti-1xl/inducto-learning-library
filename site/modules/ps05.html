Productivity Systems

A system simple enough that you will still be using it in March.

Why this matters

Latika's fourth system this year

Latika manages purchasing in Jaipur. In January she started a new app. In March she moved to a notebook. In June she tried colour-coded folders. Each lasted about three weeks, and things still slip through.

Four fresh starts, and the same forgotten commitments.
What changes

One place, one daily review, and a rule for what goes where.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Keep one place for commitments instead of six
  • 2Run a two-minute daily review you will actually do
  • 3Capture a commitment in under ten seconds
  • 4Recognise why your last system stopped working
  • 5Recover from a week where you abandoned it entirely
Why systems fail

Complexity, not discipline

Complexity, not discipline

People blame themselves for abandoning a system. Usually the system was asking for more than any working week allows.

Systems that fail

Too much structure

  • Projects, contexts, energy levels and tags
  • A weekly review that takes ninety minutes
  • Three apps that need to stay in sync
  • Perfect for two weeks, abandoned in the third
Systems that last

Almost no structure

  • One list, one file, one place
  • A review that takes two minutes
  • Works on paper if the laptop dies
  • Survives a bad week without collapsing

One place for everything

The capture rule

One place, always

Terms on this page: Capture

Where commitments actually arrive

In corridors

"Can you look at that by Friday?" Half of all commitments are made standing up.

In meetings

Agreed verbally, minuted vaguely, remembered differently by everyone present.

In messages

Buried in a thread you will not find again in three days.

The two-minute review

Daily, not weekly

Two minutes, daily

A weekly review is a good idea that most people never do twice. A daily one takes two minutes and survives.

Copy these four questions
1. What did I commit to today that is not written down? 2. What is overdue that I have quietly stopped looking at? 3. What is the one thing that must move tomorrow? 4. What can I strike off entirely?
Ask these at the same point every day. The end of the day works best.
Why this works
  • Question one catches corridor commitments before they vanish.
  • Question two is the one people avoid, and the most useful.
  • Question four is why the list does not grow forever.

Daily beats weekly

The weekly review
  • Ninety minutes, scheduled for Friday afternoon.
  • Skipped whenever Friday is busy, which is most Fridays.
  • After two skips the list is stale and gets abandoned.
The daily two minutes
  • Four questions, at the end of the day, every day.
  • Skipping one day costs nothing at all.
  • The list is never more than a day out of date.

A system's real quality is how well it survives the week you ignore it.

When it collapses

Restarting without guilt

Restarting without guilt

  1. Do not read the old list. It is stale and it will depress you.
  2. Write down everything on your mind right now, in five minutes.
  3. Then skim the old list and move across only what is still live.
  4. Delete the rest. Nothing bad has ever come of this.
Copy this prompt
Here is a long, messy list of things I have committed to. Sort it into: OVERDUE, THIS WEEK, NOT NOW. Put each item in exactly one group. Then tell me the three I should do first. No commentary.
Use this when the list has become a source of guilt rather than a tool.

The list is not the work

Time spent organising the list is not time spent doing the work.

Tidying a system is the most convincing form of procrastination there is.

  • Two minutes a day is the budget.
  • New app is almost never the answer.
  • If it is tidy and nothing moved, that is a bad day.
Do this now

Set up the minimum

Do this now: set the minimum

  1. Pick one place. A note on your phone is genuinely fine.
  2. Write down every commitment you can remember making.
  3. Set a daily reminder for the four review questions.
  4. Do it tomorrow, even if you write nothing new down.
Copy this prompt
Ask me the four review questions one at a time, wait for my answer to each, and at the end give me back a short list of what I said. Do not add advice, do not add tasks I did not mention, and do not ask about anything else.
Useful on the days you cannot face the list alone.

What goes where

Does this have a fixed time attached to it?
Yes

Calendar

Meetings, deadlines, protected blocks and anything that must happen at a particular moment. If it is not in the calendar, it does not exist.

No

The list

Everything else. Tasks, commitments, things to chase, things to read. One list, sorted by consequence rather than by date.

Four habits worth having

Five mistakes people really make

1
Starting a new app when the old one fails
The app was never the problem. The setup cost buys three more weeks and the same collapse.
2
Sorting at the moment of capture
Ten seconds becomes ninety, so you stop capturing at all.
3
Keeping commitments in your head
The ones made in corridors are exactly the ones that get forgotten.
4
A weekly review that takes ninety minutes
It gets skipped twice and then abandoned. Daily and tiny survives.
5
Treating an out-of-date list as a failure
Every list goes stale. Restarting takes five minutes and no guilt is required.

The productivity rule

The best system is the one still running in March.

Simplicity is not a compromise here. It is the whole design requirement.

  • One place to capture.
  • Two minutes to review.
  • Restart without guilt.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Capture
Writing a commitment down the moment it is made, in under ten seconds.
Daily review
Four questions at the same point each day. Two minutes, and it survives bad weeks.
Stale list
A list nobody has opened for days. Restart from memory rather than reading it.
Consequence
What breaks if a task slips. A better sort order than urgency or date.
Protected block
Time booked in the calendar for work needing concentration.
Inbox
A list of other people's priorities, sorted by when they were sent.
