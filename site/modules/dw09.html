Brainstorming with AI

Twenty options in two minutes, and a way to tell which three are actually worth your time.

Why this matters

The same four ideas, every time

A cost-reduction meeting in Ahmedabad runs every month. The same four ideas come up, in the same order, from the same three people. Everyone knows they are not enough. Nobody arrives with a fifth.

Twelve meetings a year, four ideas, none of them new.
What changes

Twenty starting points in two minutes, so the room argues instead of stalling.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Get twenty usable starting points in under two minutes
  • 2Ask for bad ideas on purpose, and see why it works
  • 3Sort twenty ideas down to three without a long debate
  • 4Use it to break a deadlock between two entrenched views
  • 5Recognise the ideas it produces that are quietly impossible
Quantity first

Why twenty beats four

Twenty beats four

Meetings stall because people arrive with their best idea, not with twenty rough ones.

1

Four ideas in the room

Each one somebody's, so each one gets defended.

2

Twenty on a page

Nobody owns them, so nobody defends them.

3

The room sorts

Arguing about ideas, not about people.

4

Three get tested

Usually including one nobody had thought of.

Nobody owns a generated idea

Ideas people brought

Attached to a person

  • Rejecting it feels like rejecting them
  • The senior person's idea wins by default
  • People bring one idea, well defended
  • Quiet people bring none at all
Ideas on a list

Attached to nobody

  • Anyone can dismiss any of them freely
  • Seniority stops deciding which survive
  • Twenty options, none of them precious
  • Quiet people argue as easily as loud ones
Asking for range

Safe, odd and impossible

Ask for the bad ones too

Copy this prompt
Give me twenty ways to reduce packaging cost in a warehouse. Number them. Include five that are obvious, ten that are unusual, and five that are probably impractical. Do not explain them and do not recommend any. One line each.
The five impractical ones are the point, not padding.
Why this works
  • Impractical ideas are where the unusual ones come from.
  • "Do not recommend any" stops it filtering for you.
  • One line each keeps it scannable in the meeting.

Why impossible ideas help

Asking for good ideas
  • "Give me ten good ways to reduce packaging cost."
  • You get ten sensible, familiar suggestions.
  • Nine of them were already on the whiteboard last month.
Asking for range
  • "Five obvious, ten unusual, five probably impractical."
  • The impractical ones include "stop packaging entirely for local deliveries".
  • That is impractical everywhere except two routes, where it works.

Nobody says an impossible thing out loud in a meeting. That is exactly why the good version of it never gets found.

Sorting fast

Twenty down to three

Twenty down to three

The sorting prompt

Copy this sorting prompt
Sort the twenty ideas below into three groups. GROUP 1: could be tested by one person within a week. GROUP 2: would need budget or approval. GROUP 3: not worth pursuing. Put every idea in exactly one group. Do not explain and do not add new ideas.
Group 1 is where you start, every time.
What comes back
  • Three clean groups, with group one usually holding four or five ideas.
  • No commentary, so the meeting argues about the sorting rather than reading.
  • Twenty ideas triaged in about thirty seconds.
Breaking a deadlock

When two people are stuck

When two people are stuck

Deadlocks are rarely about the options. They are about two people having stated a position out loud.

Copy this deadlock prompt
Two people disagree. Option A is to insource the packing line. Option B is to renegotiate with the current vendor. Give me five options that are neither A nor B, and two ways of combining A and B. One line each, no recommendation.
Seven new things to argue about, none of them anybody's position.
Why this works
  • It moves the argument off the two stated positions.
  • Combinations are what neither side proposes on their own.
  • Nobody has to lose to accept a third option.

Ideas that are quietly impossible

It does not know our contracts, our plant or our regulator.

Some ideas will be excellent and completely unavailable to us.

  • Things our supplier contract already prohibits
  • Things the site licence or regulator does not permit
  • Things requiring equipment we do not have
  • Things another team tried and rejected last year
Do this now

Generate on a real problem

Do this now: generate twenty

  1. Take a problem your team keeps circling.
  2. Write it in one sentence, with no names or figures.
  3. Run the twenty-ideas prompt on it.
  4. Strike everything already tried, then sort what is left.
Copy this prompt
Give me twenty ways to [PROBLEM IN ONE SENTENCE]. Number them. Five obvious, ten unusual, five probably impractical. One line each. Do not explain them, do not recommend any, and do not group them.
Two minutes. Bring the page to the meeting instead of your one idea.

Four habits for idea sessions

Five mistakes people really make

1
Asking for "good ideas"
You get the familiar ones. The useful ones live next to the impractical ones.
2
Letting it recommend an option
It has no view worth having. Recommending is the part the room is for.
3
Treating the list as a plan
Twenty ideas is raw material. Nothing on it has been checked against reality.
4
Describing the problem with real figures
Cost structures and margins do not need to leave the company to generate ideas.
5
Generating in the meeting itself
Everyone reads instead of thinking. Bring the list already made.

When brainstorming is the wrong tool

Is the problem a shortage of options, or of agreement?
Options

Generate

You keep circling the same four ideas and none of them is good enough. Twenty rough starting points is exactly what is missing.

Agreement

Decide instead

Everybody knows what should happen and nobody will commit. More options make that worse. What is needed is a decision and an owner.

What good use looks like

Twenty ideas, generated alone

Two minutes, before anybody is in the room.

Struck down to eight

Already tried, not affordable, not permitted.

One tested this week

By one person, with a date.

The tool contributes the first layer only. Everything that makes it useful happens after that, in the room and on the floor.

The brainstorming rule

Use it for quantity. Use the room for judgement.

It is genuinely good at producing options and has no idea which of them we can actually do.

  • Twenty options — its job.
  • Striking and sorting — yours.
  • Testing one — this week.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Divergent thinking
Producing many options without judging them. The part AI does well.
Convergent thinking
Narrowing many options down to a decision. The part the room does.
Constraint
Something that rules an option out: a contract, a licence, a budget, a piece of equipment.
Prompt
Everything you type in: the problem, the range you want and the format.
Output
What comes back. Raw material, none of which has been checked against reality.
Deadlock
Two stated positions and no movement. Usually social rather than analytical.
