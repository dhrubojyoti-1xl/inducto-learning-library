Phishing & Social Engineering

How people get talked into handing over the password, the payment or the file — and the pause that stops it.

Why this matters

Rekha pays the wrong bank account

Rekha handles accounts payable in Gurugram. An email arrives from a regular supplier saying their bank details have changed. The signature, the logo and the invoice format are all correct. She updates the record and pays.

One payment, to an account nobody at that supplier controls.
What changes

One phone call to a number you already had. That is the whole defence.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Name the four pressure signals that appear in almost every attack
  • 2Use one verification habit that defeats a convincing email
  • 3Recognise an attack that arrives by phone or WhatsApp
  • 4Know why a real-looking sender proves nothing at all
  • 5Report a suspected attempt without worrying about being wrong
It is not about spelling

Modern attacks look perfect

They do not look wrong any more

The badly spelled email is twenty years out of date. Modern attempts are copied from real correspondence.

What we teach people

Look for the mistakes

  • Poor spelling and odd grammar
  • A strange-looking email address
  • Generic greetings like "Dear Customer"
  • Obviously fake logos
What actually arrives

Copied from the real thing

  • Perfect English, often AI-written
  • A real display name, sometimes a real address
  • Your name, your order number, your supplier
  • The exact logo and invoice layout

A sender name proves nothing

1

The name is typed in

Display names are free text. Anyone can put anything there.

2

The address can be close

One character different, or a lookalike domain.

3

Replies go elsewhere

The reply-to address can differ from the one shown.

4

So looking is not checking

Only a separate channel actually verifies.

The four pressure signals

What every attack needs

Four signals in every attack

Attacks do not need you to be careless. They need you to be busy and to feel pushed.

Tick each one only once you are sure you never do it.

One is enough to make you check

A change, on its own, is reason enough

New bank details, a new process, a new address — verify that alone, even with no urgency and no pressure attached to it.

Urgency plus authority

"The director needs this paid before five." A common combination, and an easy one to notice.

Secrecy plus a change

"Do not mention this yet — the new account is confidential." Nothing legitimate needs both.

The verification habit

One call, a number you had

One call, a number you had

Verifying inside the message
  • You ring the number in the email signature.
  • You reply to the email asking "is this really you?"
  • Both reach whoever sent it. Confirmation is worthless.
Verifying outside it
  • You ring the number already in your contacts or on the invoice you had before.
  • You walk to the person's desk, or message them on Teams.
  • Any channel they did not choose defeats the whole attempt.

The rule is not "verify". It is "verify somewhere the sender does not control".

The wording, ready to use

Copy this wording
Hello, I have an email asking me to change the bank details on your account. Before I touch anything I need to confirm it with you directly. Can you confirm whether that request came from your side? I am calling the number we already had on file.
Nobody legitimate has ever been offended by this call.
Why this works
  • It states plainly that you are using an old number.
  • It asks a yes-or-no question they can answer in seconds.
  • It costs two minutes and stops the entire attack.
Phone, WhatsApp and in person

Not just email

Not only email

1
A call from "IT support"
Asking you to read out a code or install a remote-access tool. Nobody real does this.
2
A WhatsApp from your manager's "new number"
Urgent, brief, and asking for a payment or a gift card. Ring the old number.
3
A visitor who follows you through the door
Holding boxes and looking rushed. Politely ask them to sign in at reception.
4
A supplier asking for a document "resent"
Testing whether you will send a file to a new address without checking.

The code that is never shared

Nobody legitimate will ever ask you to read out a one-time code.

Not IT, not the bank, not a director, not a supplier. There is no exception.

  • A code on your phone stays on your phone.
  • Anyone asking for it is attacking you.
  • Report it, even if you did not give it.
Do this now

Test one real message

Do this now: test one message

  1. Open the last message that asked you to do something financial.
  2. Count how many of the four pressure signals it contains.
  3. Find a number for the sender that did not come from the message.
  4. If you cannot find one independently, that is your answer.
Copy this prompt
I received a message asking me to act. Ask me six short questions, one at a time, to work out whether it shows signs of a phishing or impersonation attempt. Do not ask me to paste the message. At the end give me a verdict and one action.
Describe the message. Never paste it, and never paste any attachment.

If you already clicked

Reporting a false alarm is free

Am I sure enough to be worth someone's time?
Not sure

Report it anyway

A false alarm costs somebody two minutes. A real attempt that nobody reported costs considerably more, and usually arrives at four other people's desks as well.

Certain it is fake

Report it and delete

Reporting matters even when you spotted it. If it reached you, it reached colleagues, and some of them are busier than you were.

Why smart people get caught

It arrives when you are busy

Late afternoon, month end, during a deadline.

It looks completely routine

A supplier you know, an invoice you expected.

It asks for one small thing

Update a field, confirm a code, resend a file.

Nobody is caught by an obvious scam. People are caught by an ordinary request on a busy afternoon, which is exactly what these are designed to be.

The phishing rule

Anything that changes where money or data goes gets verified on a channel the sender did not choose.

That single habit defeats nearly every version of this attack.

  • Bank details change — call an old number.
  • Urgent and secret — verify twice.
  • One-time code requested — report it.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Phishing
A message designed to make you hand over access, money or a document.
Social engineering
Manipulating a person rather than attacking a system. The pressure signals are the tools.
Spoofing
Making a message appear to come from somebody else. Display names are free text.
Business email compromise
An attacker using a real or lookalike account to redirect a payment.
One-time code
A short code proving it is you. Never shared with anyone, for any reason.
Out-of-band
Checking through a different channel the sender does not control.
