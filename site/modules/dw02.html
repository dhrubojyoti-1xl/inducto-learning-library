Research with AI

Using it to find the right questions fast — without letting it invent the answers.

Why this matters

Vikram has two hours to prepare

Vikram sells industrial fasteners from Ahmedabad. A meeting with a pharmaceutical client is at four. He knows nothing about pharma cleanroom requirements and does not know what he does not know.

Two hours of reading, and still the wrong questions in the room.
What changes

Ten minutes to map the subject. Then real sources for the facts.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Map an unfamiliar subject in ten minutes, not two hours
  • 2Get the questions worth asking, instead of invented answers
  • 3Know which half of research AI can do and which it cannot
  • 4Spot a confident answer that has no source behind it
  • 5Turn a real document into notes without reading every page
Two halves of research

Mapping and verifying

Research has two halves

AI is genuinely excellent at one of them and actively dangerous at the other.

Mapping

AI does this well

  • What are the main concepts here?
  • What questions should I be asking?
  • What usually goes wrong in this area?
  • What would an expert want to know first?
Verifying

AI cannot do this

  • What is the current standard number?
  • What did the regulator say last month?
  • What does this supplier actually charge?
  • Which of these claims is true today?

Why mapping is safe

1

You ask for questions

Not for answers, and not for numbers.

2

It lists concepts

Drawn from very common patterns, so broadly reliable.

3

You recognise gaps

"I have never heard of that" is the useful output.

4

You go and verify

With sources, in the half hour you just saved.

Mapping a new subject

Ten minutes, no facts needed

Ten minutes to map a subject

Copy this prompt
I am meeting a customer in an industry I do not know: pharmaceutical manufacturing. Give me three things. One: the eight terms I will hear and what each means in one line. Two: the five questions a buyer there would expect a supplier to ask. Three: the three things suppliers usually get wrong. No numbers, no standards, no prices.
"No numbers, no standards" is what keeps this safe.
Why this works
  • Terms and questions are stable, common knowledge.
  • Banning numbers removes the part it would invent.
  • You walk in able to ask, not pretending to know.

Ask for questions, not answers

Asking for answers
  • "What cleanroom standard applies to this customer?"
  • You get a specific standard number, stated plainly.
  • You repeat it in the meeting. It is the wrong one.
Asking for questions
  • "What should I ask this customer about their cleanroom requirements?"
  • You get six questions, three of which you would never have thought of.
  • You ask them. The customer tells you the real answer.

The person in the room knows. Your job was to know what to ask them.

Where facts come from

Never from the model

Facts come from sources

Terms on this page: Grounding

The invented citation

It will produce a reference that looks perfect and does not exist.

Report titles, section numbers, author names and years are all easy patterns to generate.

  • A study title with a plausible year attached
  • A section number in a real regulation
  • An industry body that sounds entirely real
  • A statistic quoted to one decimal place
Reading a long document

Grounded and safe

Forty pages, four minutes

This is grounded research: it can only describe text you actually gave it.

Copy this document prompt
Using only the document below, answer three questions. What does it require us to do? What deadlines does it set? What does it leave unclear? Quote the exact wording for anything you say it requires. If something is not in the document, say "not stated".
"Quote the exact wording" is the line that makes this checkable.
What comes back
  • Three short answers with quoted phrases you can search for in the original.
  • "Not stated" against the things you assumed were covered.
  • Forty pages reduced to the six lines that affect you.

Check the document is yours to paste

Is this document public, or ours to keep private?
Public

Paste it

A published circular, a standard, a public tender, a manufacturer's datasheet. It is already on the open internet.

Private

Do not paste

A contract, a customer's specification, an internal policy, anything marked confidential. Read it yourself and describe what you need.

Do this now

Map something real

Do this now: map something

  1. Pick a subject you will need to sound competent in this month.
  2. Paste the prompt on the right with that subject in it.
  3. Mark every term you did not already know.
  4. Look up two of them properly, from a real source.
Copy this prompt
I need to get up to speed on [SUBJECT] for a [MEETING / REPORT] next week. Give me: eight terms and one-line definitions, five questions I should ask, and three common mistakes outsiders make. No numbers, no standards, no prices, no company names.
The last sentence is what stops it inventing.

Four habits for research

Five mistakes people really make

1
Repeating a statistic from an AI answer
It sounds researched, it convinces the room, and nobody can find the source afterwards.
2
Asking what a regulation says
It has never read your regulation. It describes a regulation that sounds like it.
3
Trusting a citation because it has a year on it
Titles, authors and years are extremely easy patterns to generate convincingly.
4
Pasting a customer's specification in
That is their confidential document, shared with us and nobody else.
5
Stopping once the answer sounds complete
Completeness is the default. It is not a signal that anything was verified.

What good research looks like

Questions first

You walk into the meeting able to ask well, not pretending to know already.

Documents pasted

Anything it says about a public document can be searched for in the original.

Facts from sources

Every number you repeat came from a page you opened, not a sentence it produced.

The research rule

Use it to find out what to ask. Never to find out what is true.

That division makes AI the fastest research assistant you have, and a safe one.

  • Questions and concepts — ask it.
  • Numbers and rules — open the source.
  • Private documents — read them yourself.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Grounding
Giving the tool the actual source text so its answer has something real to stand on.
Citation
A reference to a real document. AI produces convincing ones that do not exist.
Mapping
Learning the shape of a subject: its terms, its questions, its usual mistakes.
Cut-off date
The point where a model's training text stops. Nothing after it was ever seen.
Hallucination
A confident, invented answer. Citations and statistics are the classic cases.
Source
A document, system or person you can point at. A model is never one.
