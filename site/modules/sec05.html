Handling Confidential Information

The commercial material that is not personal data, and still must never leave the building.

Why this matters

Divya shows a slide with a real price

Divya supports the legal team in Bengaluru. She reuses an old slide in a supplier presentation. It still carries the discount structure agreed with a different supplier. Nobody notices in the room. The photograph circulates.

One supplier now knows exactly what we give another.
What changes

Four classes of information, and a habit of checking the slide.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Sort information into four classes in a few seconds
  • 2Spot confidential material hidden in reused documents
  • 3Share what a third party needs without over-sharing
  • 4Know what never leaves, regardless of who is asking
  • 5Handle the request that comes from inside the company
Four classes

Public to restricted

Four classes, few seconds

Most companies use some version of this. The names matter less than the habit of asking.

Restricted means inside too

Public

Anyone, anywhere. Already published by us.

Internal and confidential

Colleagues who need it for their work.

Restricted

A named few. Not the whole department.

The mistake people make is assuming a colleague automatically qualifies. Restricted material is restricted from most of us, which is what the word means.

Where it hides

Reused files and old slides

It hides in reused files

1
An old slide left in a reused deck
Discount structures, margins and named accounts survive in slides nobody scrolled to.
2
Hidden columns in a shared spreadsheet
Hiding a column does not remove it. Anyone can unhide it in one click.
3
Tracked changes and comments in a document
The internal argument about pricing is still in the file you sent out.
4
File properties and the file name itself
"Margin analysis v4 FINAL confidential.xlsx" tells a story before it is opened.
5
A photograph of a whiteboard
The other half of the board is usually still legible in the picture.

Check before it leaves

Sharing outside

Need to know, not nice to know

Need to know, not nice to know

Sending the whole file
  • A supplier asks for last quarter's volumes for their product.
  • You send the whole volume report, covering every product and supplier.
  • They now know what their competitors ship to us.
Sending the answer
  • You copy out the three lines that concern their product.
  • Into a fresh document, with nothing else in it.
  • They get a better answer, faster, and learn nothing else.

Sending the whole file is almost always laziness rather than generosity. The extract takes two minutes.

The extract habit

Copy this wording
Thanks — I can share the figures for your product line. I will send those as a separate extract rather than the full report, since the report covers other suppliers as well. You will have it within the hour.
Nobody has ever objected to this. Most people expect it.
Why this works
  • It says yes, which keeps the relationship easy.
  • It explains why, so it does not read as suspicion.
  • It commits to a time, so it is not a soft refusal.
Requests from inside

Colleagues are not automatic

Colleagues are not automatic

Being employed here is not the same as needing to know. Most internal leaks start as a helpful favour.

Do they need this for work they are actually doing?
Yes

Share it

Their job requires it. Send the part they need, not the whole file — exactly as you would externally.

Curious

Point them on

"That one sits with Finance." Not a refusal — it puts the decision with whoever owns the data.

Saying no without friction

Copy this wording
I do not think I am the right person to share that one — it sits with [TEAM] and they will know what can go out. Happy to introduce you if that helps. If they are fine with it, send it over and I will work from it.
Redirects rather than refuses. Almost nobody pushes back.
Why this works
  • It puts the decision with whoever owns the information.
  • It offers help, so it does not read as obstruction.
  • It keeps you out of a judgement that is not yours.
Do this now

Classify five real documents

Do this now: classify five

  1. Open the five documents you have used most this week.
  2. Put each into one of the four classes, out loud.
  3. For any confidential one, check who currently has access.
  4. Remove anyone who no longer needs it.
Copy this prompt
I will describe a document without showing it to you. Ask me four short questions, then tell me whether it is public, internal, confidential or restricted, and who should be able to open it. Do not ask me to paste the contents.
Describe the type of document. Never paste the document.

What never leaves, ever

Some material does not leave regardless of who is asking or why.

If somebody insists, that itself is worth reporting.

  • Pricing or margin structures agreed with another party
  • Salaries, appraisals or disciplinary records
  • Legal advice, live disputes or draft settlements
  • Unannounced acquisitions, closures or product plans

Marking things properly

Mark it once

A single word in the header. The reader then knows without asking anybody.

Say who it is for

"Confidential — Finance and Directors only" is far more useful than "Confidential".

Say when it stops

"Restricted until the announcement on 12 May" prevents permanent over-caution.

Five mistakes people really make

1
Reusing a deck without scrolling to the end
The slide you forgot is the one somebody photographs.
2
Sending a whole report to answer one question
Generosity that hands over every other supplier's position.
3
Assuming a colleague qualifies
Employment is not need-to-know. Most internal leaks start as a favour.
4
Discussing figures in a public place
Airports, cafés and shared taxis. Somebody is always close enough.
5
Marking everything confidential
When everything is marked, nothing is. People stop reading the label.

The confidentiality rule

Send the answer, not the file it came from.

That habit covers external sharing, internal favours and reused documents all at once.

  • Extract, do not forward.
  • Need to know, not nice to know.
  • Scroll to the end before sending.

Recap

Confidential information on one screen

Four classes

Public, internal, confidential, restricted. Ask which, in a few seconds.

Restricted excludes most colleagues

Salaries, legal matters and unannounced plans are not department-wide.

It hides in reused files

Extra slides, hidden columns, tracked changes, file names, whiteboard photos.

Send the answer, not the file

Extracting three lines takes two minutes and reveals nothing else.

Employment is not need-to-know

Redirect to the owner rather than refusing. Nobody takes offence.

Do not mark everything

When every file is confidential, the label stops meaning anything.

Send the answer, not the file it came from.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Classification
Deciding which of the four classes a document belongs to, and who may open it.
Need to know
The test for sharing: does this person require it for work they are actually doing?
Restricted
Material limited to a named few, including inside the company.
Extract
A fresh document containing only the part somebody actually needs.
Metadata
Information about a file: its name, author, comments and tracked changes.
Confidential
Damaging if it leaves the company, even though no individual is identifiable.
