Instructions & Context

How much background to give — and the point where adding more starts making the answer worse.

Why this matters

Nikhil gets a plan for someone else

Nikhil coordinates projects for a Hyderabad office. He asks for "a project plan for a warehouse fit-out". He gets a polished twelve-week plan, with roles the company does not have, for a country it does not operate in.

Forty minutes deleting somebody else's assumptions.
What changes

Four lines of context, and the plan describes your project.

The four context lines

Who, what, where, why

Four lines it cannot guess

Without these it invents a company, a reader and a situation. All three will be wrong.

What each line prevents

1

No reader

It writes for a general audience and pleases nobody.

2

No business

It assumes an industry, usually American software.

3

No place

You get dollars, US law and the wrong holidays.

4

No urgency

It writes a leisurely note when you needed three lines.

Instruction or context?

Two different jobs

Instruction or context?

Context

Facts about the situation

  • "The client is in Sharjah"
  • "This is the third delay this quarter"
  • "We cannot offer a discount"
  • "Our contact there is new to the account"
Instruction

What to do with them

  • "Write a reply email"
  • "Under 120 words"
  • "Apologetic but do not accept liability"
  • "End with a question, not a statement"

Terms on this page: Context

Both, in one paragraph

Copy this prompt
Write a reply email to a client in Sharjah. Context: this is the third delay this quarter, our contact there is new to the account, and we cannot offer a discount. Instructions: under 120 words, apologetic without accepting liability, end with a question rather than a statement.
Context first, instructions second. It works as written.
Why this works
  • The two words "Context" and "Instructions" do real work.
  • Nothing about the situation is left for it to invent.
  • You can check each line of the reply against your own text.
Saying what not to do

The three usual habits

Say what not to do

Three habits show up in almost every AI draft. One line each removes them.

1
The throat-clearing opener
"I hope this email finds you well." Add: no opening pleasantries, start with the point.
2
The apology for everything
"We sincerely apologise for any inconvenience." Add: one apology only, and be specific.
3
The summary of your own question
"You asked me to write about..." Add: no preamble, no restating the request.

The three lines to reuse

Copy these three lines
Do not open with pleasantries — start with the point. Do not restate my request back to me. Use one apology at most, and make it specific rather than general.
Paste these under any writing prompt, every time.
What changes
  • The email starts with the actual news instead of a line about hoping you are well.
  • Roughly forty words disappear, and none of them were carrying meaning.
  • It reads like a person wrote it in a hurry, which is usually what you want.
How much is too much

Where it starts to hurt

Where extra context hurts

Would a new colleague need this to do the task?
Yes

Include it

If a capable new joiner could not write the email without knowing it, the tool cannot either. That is the whole test.

No

Leave it out

Extra background dilutes the instruction. The tool starts weighing detail you did not care about, and the reply drifts away from the point.

Too little, right, too much

Too little
  • "Write a reply about the delay."
  • You get a general apology letter with no dates and no position.
  • Everything that mattered was left for it to invent.
Too much
  • Four paragraphs of company history, then the request.
  • You get a reply that mentions the history and buries the new date.
  • It weighted what you spent the most words on.

It reads emphasis from volume. Whatever you write most about is what the answer will be about.

Do this now

Rewrite one real prompt

Do this now: rewrite one prompt

  1. Think of a message you sent an AI tool this week that disappointed you.
  2. Write the four context lines for it: reader, business, place, urgency.
  3. Add your instructions: length, tone, format, and what not to do.
  4. Run it again and compare the two answers side by side.
Copy this prompt
Context: you are writing for me, a project coordinator at an Indian logistics company. The reader is a site contractor who has missed two deadlines. Instructions: write a firm progress-chasing email, under 100 words, no pleasantries, end with a specific date request.
Swap in your own reader and business. The shape stays identical.

Context you should never add

Context makes answers better. Some context makes the company liable.

Everything here can be described without identifying anyone.

  • The client's name, address or phone number
  • Prices, margins or wording lifted from a contract
  • A colleague's name attached to pay or performance
  • Anything marked internal, confidential or restricted

Four habits worth keeping

Five mistakes people really make

1
Giving the task but never the reader
The tool writes for nobody in particular, which reads as a form letter to everybody.
2
Assuming it knows the country
Without a location you get dollars, US holidays and the wrong labour rules.
3
Pasting the whole email thread for context
You have now shared every name and address in it, to improve one paragraph.
4
Writing context as one long story
It weights by volume. The longest part of your prompt becomes the point of the answer.
5
Never saying what to avoid
The pleasantries, the preamble and the triple apology come back every single time.

What good context looks like

One clause per fact

"A new contact, already annoyed, in Sharjah." Three facts, eight words, no names.

Front-loaded

Context first, instructions last. The final instruction is the one it follows hardest.

Explicitly bounded

What to avoid is as useful as what to do, and far more reliable than hoping.

The context rule

Give it what a new colleague would need, and nothing a new colleague would not.

That single test settles both halves of the question.

  • They would need it — include it.
  • They would not — cut it.
  • They must not see it — never type it.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Context
The background you give so the tool knows who the text is for and why it is being written.
Instruction
What to do with that background: the task, the length, the tone, the format.
Negative instruction
Naming something you do not want, so a common habit is ruled out.
Prompt
Everything you type in: context plus instructions plus your facts.
Token
A small piece of text, roughly three quarters of a word. Long prompts are counted in these.
Output
What comes back. Your draft, which you are responsible for checking.
