Business Communication

Getting a decision out of people who are busy, in messages they read to the end.

Why this matters

Gopal writes 400 words and waits

Gopal manages operations near Nagpur. He needs a decision on overtime by Friday. He writes a careful 400-word email explaining the background, the options and the costs. By Wednesday, nobody has replied.

Four days lost, and the decision still not made.
What changes

The ask in the first line, and everything else underneath it.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Put the ask in the first line, every time
  • 2Write one message with one decision in it
  • 3Choose the right channel instead of defaulting to email
  • 4Say what happens if nobody replies, and mean it
  • 5Rewrite a message that got no response, in two minutes
Ask first

Why 400 words fail

Put the ask in line one

Most people read the first line and the last. Everything you buried in the middle was never read.

Background first
  • Four paragraphs of context, then the question.
  • The reader stops after two, meaning to come back.
  • They never come back, and you are chasing on Thursday.
Ask first
  • "I need a yes or no on weekend overtime by Friday midday."
  • Then the context, for whoever wants it.
  • The reader knows in four seconds whether this needs them.

Front-loading is not rudeness. It is the only way a busy person can triage twenty messages before a meeting.

The four-part message

1

The ask

What you need, and by when. One sentence.

2

Why it matters

The consequence if it does not happen.

3

The options

Two or three, not seven.

4

The detail

For anyone who wants it. Most will not.

One message, one ask

Two questions get one answer

Two questions get one answer

Three asks

One long message

  • Approve the overtime, confirm the shift pattern, and sign the PO
  • The reader answers the easiest one
  • You chase the other two separately anyway
  • Nobody can tell what is outstanding
One ask each

Three short messages

  • Each has one subject line and one question
  • Each can be answered in ten seconds
  • Each can be forwarded to the right person
  • You can see instantly what is still open

The message, ready to use

Copy this structure
I need [a decision / a document / an approval] on [subject] by [day and time]. If we do not have it by then, [what happens]. The options are [A] or [B]. Background is below if you need it, but the question is just A or B.
Five brackets. Fill them in and stop writing.
Why this works
  • The deadline is stated, so it is not a suggestion.
  • The consequence makes the deadline real.
  • "The question is just A or B" makes replying trivial.
Choosing the channel

Email is not always right

Email is not always right

The escalation ladder

First: a clear email

One ask, a deadline, and a stated consequence. Most things end here.

Then: a short call

After one missed deadline. Two minutes on the phone beats a third email.

Then: involve a manager

With the email trail attached, factually, without complaint or commentary.

The silent default

What happens if nobody replies

Say what silence means

A deadline with no consequence attached is a suggestion, and everybody treats it as one.

Copy this wording
If I do not hear back by Thursday 5pm, I will assume we are proceeding with option A and will book the shift accordingly. Please tell me before then if that is not what you want.
Use it only when you can genuinely act on the default.
Why this works
  • It removes the cost of not replying, which was the problem.
  • It gives the reader an easy way to stop you.
  • It converts silence into a decision instead of a delay.

When not to use a default

A silent default only works if you can honestly act on it, and if the reader could reasonably object in time.

Used wrongly it reads as a threat, or as a decision taken behind somebody's back.

  • Never for spending somebody else's budget.
  • Never with under 24 hours' notice.
  • Never for anything involving a person.
Do this now

Rewrite one real message

Do this now: rewrite one

  1. Find a message you sent that never got a reply.
  2. Delete everything except the actual question.
  3. Put the deadline and the consequence in the first line.
  4. Send it again, with a subject line naming the decision.
Copy this prompt
Rewrite the message below so the ask, the deadline and the consequence are all in the first two sentences. Move everything else below a line marked "Background". Keep it under 120 words. Do not add anything I have not written.
Two minutes, and a message people can actually answer.

The subject line does work

Weak subject
  • Subject: Overtime
  • Says nothing about what is needed or by when.
  • Sits in a list of forty other one-word subjects.
Working subject
  • Subject: Decision needed by Fri 12pm — weekend overtime, A or B
  • Readable without opening. Filterable. Findable in June.
  • The reader knows immediately whether it is theirs.

A subject line naming the decision and the deadline does more for your response rate than anything in the body.

Four habits worth having

Five mistakes people really make

1
Explaining before asking
The reader stops in paragraph two, meaning to come back. They do not come back.
2
Three questions in one email
You get an answer to the easiest one and chase the other two for a week.
3
A deadline with no consequence
It reads as a preference. Nobody has ever missed a preference and felt anything.
4
Using email for a disagreement
Tone is unreadable in text, and both sides reread it uncharitably.
5
Chasing by sending the same email again
It was not read the first time. Sending it twice mostly annoys people.

Writing for someone senior

Do they need to decide, or just to know?
Decide

Ask first

Lead with the decision, the deadline and two options. Recommend one of them — people rarely resent a recommendation, and always resent a shapeless choice.

Just know

Say so at the top

"No action needed — for information only." It takes four words and it stops a senior person reading carefully for a request that is not there.

The communication rule

Every message should answer three things in the first two sentences: what, by when, and what happens if not.

Everything else is background, and background belongs underneath.

  • What you need.
  • By when.
  • What happens if not.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Ask
The one thing you need the reader to do. It belongs in the first sentence.
Stated default
What you will do if nobody replies. It turns silence into a decision.
Channel
Email, call, chat or in person. Choosing deliberately prevents most communication problems.
Escalation
Moving a stalled request to a call, then to a manager, factually and without complaint.
Front-loading
Putting the conclusion and the request before the reasoning.
Background
Context for the few readers who want it. Always below the ask.
