Role-Based Prompts

"Act as a…" — what it actually changes, when it helps, and when it is just decoration.

Why this matters

Meera gets an answer for engineers

Meera runs quality checks at a Coimbatore plant. She asks for an explanation of a rejection trend to send to the sales team. What comes back is full of process capability indices. Sales read two lines and call her instead.

The explanation was correct and completely unusable.
What changes

One line naming the reader, and the same facts land first time.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Write a role line that changes the answer, not just the tone
  • 2Tell a useful role from a decorative one
  • 3Use the reader's role instead of the writer's, and see why
  • 4Ask the same question from three roles to stress-test a plan
  • 5Avoid the roles that invite invented authority
What a role changes

Vocabulary, depth, assumptions

What a role actually changes

It does not make the tool an expert. It shifts which words and which level of detail become likely.

1

You name a role

"Explain this to a sales manager."

2

Vocabulary shifts

Fewer technical terms, more commercial ones.

3

Depth shifts

Less method, more consequence.

4

Assumptions shift

It stops explaining what they already know.

The same facts, two readers

No role given
  • "Explain why rejections rose from 1.4 to 2.1 per cent."
  • You get capability indices, sampling method and a control chart reference.
  • Correct, and unreadable for the person who asked.
Reader named
  • "Explain this to a sales manager who will talk to the customer."
  • You get: what it means for delivery, what we are doing, what to say.
  • Same facts, ordered by what the reader has to do next.

The role did not add knowledge. It changed which parts of the answer came first.

Reader beats writer

Who receives it matters more

Name the reader, not the expert

Weaker

"Act as a quality engineer"

  • Shifts vocabulary towards jargon
  • Adds depth nobody asked for
  • Does not know who will read it
  • Sounds authoritative about our plant
Stronger

"Write this for a sales manager"

  • Shifts vocabulary towards the reader
  • Cuts detail the reader cannot use
  • Orders points by what they must do
  • Claims no authority it does not have

The reader line, ready to use

Copy this prompt
Write this for a sales manager who will speak to the customer this afternoon and has no technical background. They need to know what changed, what it means for delivery, and what we are doing about it. Under 120 words, no jargon, three short paragraphs.
Reader, purpose, then the usual instructions.
Why this works
  • "Has no technical background" removes the jargon.
  • "Will speak this afternoon" sets the urgency and length.
  • The three things they need decide the three paragraphs.
Roles that earn their place

Three that work

Three roles that work

The reader

"For a warehouse supervisor with ten minutes." Sets vocabulary, length and what to leave out.

The critic

"Read this as the customer would and list what annoys them." Finds problems you cannot see.

The checker

"As an auditor, what would you ask for evidence of?" Produces questions, not claims.

The critic role, ready to use

Copy this critic prompt
Read the message below as if you are the customer receiving it, and you are already annoyed. List the three things most likely to irritate you, in order. Do not rewrite it. Just list the three things.
"Do not rewrite it" is what makes this useful.
What comes back
  • Three specific objections, usually including one you had not considered.
  • No rewrite, so you keep control of the wording.
  • You fix two of them in a minute and ignore the third on purpose.
Roles that do nothing

Or worse than nothing

Roles that do nothing

1
"Act as a world-class expert"
Adds confident phrasing and no accuracy. It was always going to sound certain.
2
"You are a lawyer"
Produces legal-sounding text with no jurisdiction, no date and no liability behind it.
3
"Act as our CFO"
Invents the priorities of a person who exists and can be quoted back at you.
4
"Pretend you have access to our data"
It will pretend. Everything after that line is fiction presented as record.

The authority trap

A role changes the wording. It never changes what the tool actually knows.

"Act as a tax adviser" produces tax-adviser sentences, not tax advice.

  • It gains no new facts.
  • It gains no jurisdiction.
  • It gains a more confident voice.

Do this now

Three roles, one question

Do this now: three roles

  1. Take a plan or proposal you are working on.
  2. Paste it in and ask the first role question on the right.
  3. Ask the same question again as the second role, then the third.
  4. Keep every objection that appears in two of the three answers.
Copy this prompt
Read the plan below three times and answer separately each time. First as the person who has to do the work. Then as the person paying for it. Then as the customer receiving the result. Each time, list the two biggest problems you see.
One prompt, three viewpoints, six objections.

Why three viewpoints work

One viewpoint

Finds the problems you already suspected.

Three viewpoints

Finds the conflicts between them.

What survives all three

That is the objection worth fixing first.

The value is not the tool's opinion. It is being made to look at your own plan from a seat you were not sitting in.

Four habits with roles

Five mistakes people really make

1
Naming a role and nothing else
"Act as a manager" with no reader, no task and no length changes almost nothing.
2
Treating expert wording as expert judgement
It sounds more certain, which is the opposite of what you needed.
3
Role-playing a named colleague
You are inventing the views of someone who can be shown the transcript.
4
Asking a role to confirm your plan
It will agree. Ask it to attack the plan instead.
5
Using a professional role for real advice
Legal, tax and medical answers need a person who carries the liability.

When to skip the role entirely

Does the reader change what should be said?
Yes

Name the reader

Explanations, proposals, escalations, training notes. Who receives it decides the vocabulary, the order and the length.

No

Skip it

Reformatting a table, shortening a paragraph, fixing grammar. A role adds words to your prompt and nothing to the answer.

The role rule

Name who is reading it. Never claim who wrote it.

The first makes the answer usable. The second makes it sound trustworthy without being any more true.

  • Reader named — better answer.
  • Critic named — better questions.
  • Expert claimed — false confidence.

Recap

Role prompts on one screen

A role shifts words, not knowledge

Vocabulary, depth and assumptions change. What it knows does not.

Name the reader first

Who receives it decides the language, the order and the length.

Say what they must do next

"Will speak to the customer at 11" sets urgency and shape in one clause.

Use roles to attack, not to agree

"List what annoys you about this" is worth far more than approval.

Three seats beat one

Ask as doer, payer and receiver. Fix whatever appears in two of three.

Never impersonate a colleague

You would be inventing the position of someone who can read the transcript.

Name who is reading it. Never claim who wrote it.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Role prompt
A line naming who the answer is written by or for, which shifts vocabulary and depth.
Persona
Another word for the role you give the tool. It changes style, never knowledge.
Context
The background you give so the tool knows the situation it is writing about.
Prompt
Everything you type in: context plus instructions plus your facts.
Hallucination
A confident, invented answer. A role makes these sound more authoritative, not less likely.
Output
What comes back. Your draft, which you are responsible for checking.
