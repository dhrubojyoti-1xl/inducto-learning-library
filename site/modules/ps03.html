English for the Workplace

The handful of patterns that cause almost every misunderstanding at work — and how to sidestep them.

Why this matters

Sneha's update is read two ways

Sneha supervises a line near Pune. She writes: "The machine is running since morning." Her manager reads it as "it has been running all morning". The night supervisor reads it as "it started this morning after being down".

Two people planning around two different situations.
What changes

Six patterns, and a habit of writing one idea per sentence.

One idea per sentence

The single biggest win

One idea per sentence

Long sentences are where meaning gets lost. This is true in every language, and it is the fastest thing to fix.

One long sentence
  • "As the material which was ordered last week has not yet arrived and the line is scheduled for Thursday we may need to reschedule unless it comes tomorrow."
  • Thirty-four words, four ideas, one breath.
  • The reader has to work out what depends on what.
Four short ones
  • "The material ordered last week has not arrived."
  • "The line is scheduled for Thursday."
  • "If it does not come tomorrow, we will reschedule."

Nobody has ever complained that a work email was too easy to read.

The full-stop test

1

Read your sentence aloud

In your head is enough.

2

Did you need a breath?

If yes, it is too long.

3

Find the and, or which

That is usually where it splits.

4

Put a full stop there

Two clear sentences beat one clever one.

Tense and time

Where the confusion lives

Where the confusion lives

Ambiguous

Two readings possible

  • "The machine is running since morning"
  • "I am working on this from yesterday"
  • "We are waiting the material"
  • "He is having the file"
Clear

One reading only

  • "The machine has been running since 6am"
  • "I started this yesterday and I am still on it"
  • "We are waiting for the material"
  • "He has the file"

Add the time, remove the doubt

When in doubt about tense, add the actual time. A timestamp fixes almost any ambiguity.

Six patterns

The ones that cost time

Six patterns that cost time

1
"Revert" for "reply"
Outside South Asia, revert means go back to a previous state. It confuses customers.
2
"Prepone"
Not used outside South Asia at all. Say "move earlier" or "bring forward".
3
"Discuss about" and "reply back"
The extra word is redundant. "Discuss" and "reply" carry it already.
4
"Same" as a noun
"Please send the same" leaves the reader guessing. Name the thing.
5
Missing "the" and "a"
"Send report to client" reads as a telegram. Two small words soften it a great deal.

The date format problem

03/04 means 3 April here and 4 March to plenty of our customers.

We work across India, the UAE and clients elsewhere. Write the month as a word.

  • Write "3 April 2026".
  • Never 03/04/26.
  • Add the day name for deadlines.
Polite without padding

Short is not rude

Short is not rude

Politeness comes from what you offer, not from how many words you use to ask.

Padded

Longer, and less clear

  • "It would be greatly appreciated if you could kindly..."
  • "I was wondering whether it might be possible..."
  • "Please do the needful at the earliest"
  • The request is now hard to find
Direct and warm

Shorter, and friendlier

  • "Could you send the revised quote by Thursday?"
  • "Would Friday work instead?"
  • "Thanks — this one is holding up the install."
  • The request and the reason are both visible

Three phrases that carry warmth

Copy these phrases
"Thanks for turning this around quickly." / "Happy to talk it through if that is easier." / "Let me know if the date is a problem and we will work around it." / "No rush on this one — end of next week is fine."
One of these at the end does the work of a paragraph at the start.
Why this works
  • Each offers something rather than performing politeness.
  • They work in every register, from supplier to director.
  • They are short, so they do not bury the request.
Do this now

Proofread something real

Do this now: proofread one

  1. Take an email you are about to send.
  2. Read it aloud, quietly. Mark anywhere you needed a breath.
  3. Split those sentences at the "and" or the "which".
  4. Replace every vague time with a real one.
Copy this prompt
Rewrite the text below into short sentences, one idea each. Keep my meaning and my facts exactly. Replace vague times with the specific ones I have given. Do not make it more formal and do not add anything. Plain business English.
"Do not make it more formal" is the important instruction.

The one-minute proofread

Using tools without losing yourself

Does the corrected version still sound like me?
Yes

Send it

Grammar tools and AI are genuinely useful for catching slips. If the meaning and the voice are yours, the help was worth having.

No

Pull it back

If it now reads like somebody else, change three words back. A message that does not sound like you is harder to have a conversation about.

Confidence, not perfection

Clear beats correct

A short sentence with one small error is far better than a perfect one nobody can parse.

Nobody is counting

Colleagues remember whether you were clear and on time, not whether an article was missing.

It improves by writing

Six months of short sentences does more than any grammar course.

The English rule

One idea per sentence, a real time instead of a vague one, and the month spelled out.

Three habits that remove most of the misunderstandings work English causes.

  • One idea, one full stop.
  • Real times, not vague ones.
  • Months as words.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Ambiguity
A sentence with two possible meanings. The main cost of unclear writing at work.
Tense
How a verb shows time. Adding a clock time removes most tense problems entirely.
Padding
Extra words added for politeness that make the request harder to find.
Register
How formal a message is. Plain and warm travels better than heavy formality.
Handover
A note written for the person taking over. Read tired, so written short.
Article
"The" and "a". Small words that make instructions read less abrupt.
