Planning with AI

Turning a long list of intentions into a week you can actually finish.

Why this matters

Arun's list gets longer every week

Arun runs production planning near Coimbatore. His to-do list has 47 items. Every Friday he adds six and closes four. He is working hard and falling behind, and he cannot see which of the 47 actually matter.

A list that grows faster than it shrinks, indefinitely.
What changes

Sort by what breaks if it slips. Then plan only three days ahead.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of this module

  • 1Sort a long list by consequence instead of by feeling
  • 2Plan a realistic day instead of an optimistic one
  • 3Identify the tasks worth deliberately not doing
  • 4Break a stuck task into a first step you can start today

Sorting by consequence

Not by urgency or feeling

Sort by what breaks

Urgency is a feeling and it is usually somebody else's. Consequence is a fact you can check.

Sorting by urgency

How lists usually get done

  • Whoever asked most recently wins
  • Loud requests beat important ones
  • Quick wins get done to feel productive
  • The one that matters most has no deadline yet
Sorting by consequence

What actually breaks

  • A line stops if this slips
  • A customer notices if this slips
  • A colleague is blocked until this is done
  • Nothing happens at all if this slips

The consequence prompt

Copy this prompt
Below is my task list. Sort every item into four groups. STOPS SOMETHING: work halts if this slips. CUSTOMER NOTICES: someone outside sees the delay. BLOCKS A COLLEAGUE: somebody is waiting on me. NO CONSEQUENCE: nothing happens this month if it slips. Put each item in exactly one group. No commentary.
Paste your real list underneath. It can be as messy as it is.
Why this works
  • Four groups is enough to decide and few enough to scan.
  • "No consequence" is usually a third of any long list.
  • It sorts without knowing your job, because you named the tests.
Planning three days

Not five, and never a month

Plan three days, not five

1

Monday is knowable

You know roughly what will land.

2

Wednesday is a guess

Half of it will be replaced by something new.

3

Friday is fiction

Nothing planned on Monday survives to Friday intact.

4

So plan three

And re-plan on Wednesday, in five minutes.

A realistic day

Copy this day-planning prompt
Here are my tasks for tomorrow with rough time estimates. I have six working hours after meetings. Tell me which of these fit, in what order, and which do not fit at all. Assume every estimate is 50 per cent optimistic. Do not suggest working longer.
"Assume estimates are 50 per cent optimistic" is the useful line.
What comes back
  • A realistic order for three or four tasks, not nine.
  • An explicit list of what will not happen tomorrow.
  • The uncomfortable but accurate answer you would not have written yourself.
The stuck task

Breaking it into a first step

The task that never starts

Tasks that sit on a list for weeks are almost always unclear rather than hard.

Copy this unsticking prompt
This task has been on my list for three weeks and I keep not starting it: [describe the task in one line]. Give me the first five minutes of it as a single concrete action, and tell me what information I would need before I could finish it.
Five minutes, one action, no plan.
Why this works
  • A three-week-old task usually has a missing input, not a size problem.
  • A five-minute first step is small enough to actually begin.
  • Naming the missing information often reveals it belongs to somebody else.

Why tasks stick

It is not a task

"Sort out the vendor issue" is a project. Nothing starts because nothing is defined.

It needs somebody else

You are waiting for information and have not asked for it. Ask today.

You do not want to

A difficult conversation. The five-minute step is usually writing the first sentence.

Deciding not to do things

The list you keep separately

The not-doing list

Moved, not deleted

One list of 47
  • Every item you have ever thought of, in one place.
  • You reread all 47 every morning to find the four that matter.
  • The list is a source of guilt rather than a tool.
Two lists
  • Nine active items, sorted by consequence.
  • Thirty-eight in a "not now" file you look at monthly.
  • Nothing was deleted, and the morning takes ninety seconds.

Moving something to "not now" is a decision. Leaving it on the main list is avoiding one.

Do this now

Sort your real list

Do this now: sort your list

  1. Copy your actual task list into a blank document.
  2. Remove customer names and replace them with roles.
  3. Run the consequence prompt on it.
  4. Move everything in NO CONSEQUENCE to a separate file.
Copy this prompt
Sort my list into four groups: STOPS SOMETHING, CUSTOMER NOTICES, BLOCKS A COLLEAGUE, NO CONSEQUENCE. Each item in exactly one group. Then tell me the three items I should do first and why, in one line each. No other commentary.
Two minutes, and most lists shrink by a third.

The five-minute weekly review

Five mistakes people really make

1
Planning a full week in detail
By Wednesday half of it is obsolete, so the whole plan gets abandoned rather than adjusted.
2
Sorting by urgency
Urgency belongs to whoever shouted last. Consequence belongs to the work.
3
Keeping one list of everything
You reread forty items every morning to find the four that matter.
4
Trusting your own time estimates
Almost everyone is around fifty per cent optimistic, consistently.
5
Letting a task sit unstarted for weeks
It is not hard, it is undefined. Ask for the first five minutes.

What not to paste into a planner

A task list is a surprisingly detailed picture of what the company is doing.

Sorting works just as well with roles instead of names.

  • Customer names against deals or complaints
  • Colleague names against performance issues
  • Unannounced projects, sites or product plans
  • Anything from a contract, tender or board paper

Planning is not the work

Have I re-planned this week more than twice?
Yes

Stop planning

Re-planning feels productive and produces nothing. If the list is sorted and the first task is clear, the problem is starting, not planning.

No

Plan and go

Five minutes on Monday, five on Wednesday. That is the whole system, and anything more is usually avoidance.

The planning rule

Sort by what breaks. Plan three days. Decide what you will not do.

Three habits, about ten minutes a week, and the list stops growing faster than it shrinks.

  • Consequence, not urgency.
  • Three days, not five.
  • Not-now, not never.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Consequence
What actually happens if a task slips. The only reliable way to sort a long list.
Not-now list
A separate file for items with no consequence, reviewed monthly rather than daily.
Planning horizon
How far ahead you can usefully plan. Around three days for most operational roles.
First action
The concrete five minutes that starts a stuck task.
Prompt
Everything you type in: your list and the sorting tests you want applied.
Output
What comes back. A suggested order, which you still decide on.
