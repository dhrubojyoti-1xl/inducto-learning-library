Professional Email Writing

The mechanics — subject lines, openings, tone, and knowing when not to send at all.

Why this matters

Pooja's email starts a fight

Pooja handles customer service in Kochi. She writes "As per my previous email, kindly do the needful urgently." She means it neutrally. The customer reads it as sarcasm and escalates to her director.

A routine query turned into a complaint about her.
What changes

Four phrases to retire, and one structure that never reads badly.

The opening line

Delete the first sentence

Delete the first sentence

The first sentence of most work emails carries no information. Try deleting it and see if anything is lost.

Throat-clearing
  • "I hope this email finds you well."
  • "I am writing to you regarding the below matter."
  • "Further to our earlier correspondence on this subject..."
Straight in
  • "Your order 4471 will now arrive on 15 March."
  • "I need your approval on the revised quote by Thursday."
  • "Two cartons were damaged. We are replacing them this week."

Warmth comes from what you say and how you close, not from a sentence everybody skips.

The four-part email

1

The point

What has happened, or what you need.

2

The detail

Dates, numbers, references. Short.

3

The ask

One thing, with a date on it.

4

The close

Where the warmth goes, in one line.

Phrases to retire

They read worse than you mean

Four phrases to retire

1
"As per my previous email"
Reads as an accusation, whatever you intended. Say "following up on" instead.
2
"Kindly do the needful"
Says nothing about what you actually want done. Name the action and the date.
3
"Please revert"
In most of the world this means go back to a previous state. Say "please reply".
4
"Urgent" in every subject line
It stops meaning anything after the second use, and hides the ones that are.

Say what you want instead

Vague

Reader has to guess

  • "Kindly do the needful"
  • "Please look into this"
  • "Awaiting your revert"
  • "Treat as most urgent"
Specific

Reader knows exactly

  • "Please approve the revised quote"
  • "Please confirm the new date"
  • "A yes or no by Thursday would help"
  • "We need this before the 3pm dispatch"
Tone when annoyed

Neutral is a skill

Neutral is a skill

When you are annoyed, the email you write will read angrier than you meant. Every time.

The neutral chaser

Copy this wording
Following up on the revised drawings. These were due on the 8th and we have not received them yet. The install team is booked for the 19th, so we would need them by the 14th at the latest. Could you confirm a date today?
Two missed deadlines, and not one word of heat in it.
Why this works
  • Dates do the work that adjectives would have done badly.
  • No "you" appears, so nobody is being accused.
  • It ends with a small, easy request rather than a complaint.
When not to send

Three situations

Three times not to send

When you are angry

Write it, save it, reread it after lunch. You will change three things, every time.

When it is about a person

Performance, conduct, capability. Those conversations happen face to face.

When it needs a discussion

If it will take four emails, it is a five-minute call you have not made yet.

The reply-all question

Does everyone on this thread need my reply?
Yes

Reply all

A decision the group is waiting on, or information that stops others duplicating work. Rare, and genuinely useful when it applies.

No

Reply to sender

"Thanks", "Noted", or anything only the sender needs. Twelve people receiving "Thanks" is twelve interruptions for nothing.

Do this now

Fix one real email

Do this now: fix one email

  1. Open a sent email you were slightly unhappy with.
  2. Delete the first sentence and see whether anything is lost.
  3. Remove every "you" that assigns blame, and every adverb.
  4. Check the subject line names the decision and the date.
Copy this prompt
Rewrite this email to be neutral in tone. Remove any wording that assigns blame, delete adverbs, and state the facts with dates instead. Keep the same meaning and the same request. Under 100 words. Do not add an apology.
Use this on anything you wrote while irritated.

The ten-second check

Five mistakes people really make

1
Writing while annoyed and sending immediately
It always reads sharper than you meant, and it is on the record forever.
2
Reply-all with "Thanks"
Twelve interruptions for a message eleven of them did not need.
3
Marking everything urgent
The flag stops meaning anything, including on the one that genuinely is.
4
Burying the ask in paragraph three
The reader stopped at paragraph two and meant to come back.
5
Copying somebody's manager to apply pressure
It is read as an escalation, and it usually ends cooperation immediately.

The close does the warmth

Copy these closes
"Thanks — shout if anything is unclear." / "Happy to talk it through if that is easier." / "Thanks for turning this around quickly." / "Let me know if the date is a problem and we will work around it."
One line at the end does more than three at the start.
Why this works
  • It arrives after the reader has the information.
  • It offers something, rather than performing politeness.
  • It works even when the news in the middle was bad.

Writing across cultures

Our email goes between India, the UAE and clients elsewhere. Plain and specific travels best.

Idiom, sarcasm and heavy formality all read differently in different offices.

  • Short sentences, one idea each.
  • Dates written in full, never 03/04.
  • No idiom, no jokes about delays.

The email rule

Say the thing, give the date, make one ask, and put the warmth at the end.

That order works for good news, bad news and everything routine in between.

  • Point first.
  • One ask, one date.
  • Warmth in the close.

Recap

Email writing on one screen

Delete the first sentence

It almost always carries no information. Start with the point.

Retire four phrases

"As per my previous", "do the needful", "revert", "urgent" everywhere.

Warmth goes at the end

One line in the close does more than three in the opening.

Dates instead of adverbs

"Due on the 8th" lands harder than "repeatedly delayed".

Never send angry

Write it, wait thirty minutes, and change the three things you will spot.

Reply-all almost never

Only when the whole group is genuinely waiting on your answer.

Say the thing, give the date, make one ask, and put the warmth at the end.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Throat-clearing
An opening sentence that carries no information. Usually safe to delete.
Tone
How a message reads, as distinct from what it says. Text reads sharper than speech.
Escalation
Involving somebody's manager. A real step, and rarely the second one.
Reply all
Sending to everyone on a thread. Appropriate only when all of them need it.
Close
The final line, where warmth belongs. It is read; the opening often is not.
Ask
The one action you need, with a date attached.
