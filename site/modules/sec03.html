Multi-Factor Authentication

The second lock that makes a stolen password almost useless — and the one way people still get past it.

Why this matters

Ayesha's password worked. It failed.

Ayesha handles HR records in Dubai. Her password leaked in a breach at a site she had forgotten about. Someone used it at 2am. They got the password right and still could not get in, because a code went to her phone.

Nothing at all — which is the entire point of the story.
What changes

One extra step at login, and a stolen password stops being enough.

What a second factor is

Something you have

Something you know, something you have

Security people group proof of identity into three kinds: something you know (a password), something you have (your phone or a security key), and something you are (a fingerprint or your face). MFA means adding a second kind — in the systems you use day to day, usually something you have.

1

You enter the password

Something you know. It may already have leaked.

2

A second proof is asked for

Something you have, usually your phone.

3

Only you can supply it

Because the phone is in your pocket, not in a leaked list.

4

A stolen password fails

Which is the whole point of the second step.

Why it matters so much

Password only

One thing between them and us

  • A leaked password works immediately
  • You have no idea it has been used
  • It works from anywhere in the world
  • It keeps working until you change it
Password plus a factor

Two things, one of them physical

  • A leaked password alone achieves nothing
  • You are told the moment somebody tries
  • They would need the phone in your pocket
  • You find out and change it the same day
Which type is strongest

Not all are equal

Not all factors are equal

Terms on this page: Authenticator app

Any factor beats none

Use the best offered

If the system supports an app or a key, choose that over SMS. It takes two minutes to set up.

SMS still helps

Weakest is not useless. A texted code still defeats an attacker holding only your password.

Never turn it off

The inconvenience is a few seconds. The protection is against the most common attack there is.

The fatigue attack

The one way past it

The one way past it

If they have your password, they can trigger approval prompts on your phone. Over and over.

1
Prompts arrive at 2am, repeatedly
The attacker is logging in with your password every few seconds, hoping you tap once.
2
You tap approve to stop the buzzing
That single tap is the entire breach. It is the only step they could not do themselves.
3
A call follows saying it is IT
"We are testing, please approve the next one." IT will never ask this.
4
You approve while half asleep
Which is exactly why the prompts arrive at 2am rather than at 2pm.

What to do instead

Never approve a prompt you did not personally trigger, no matter how many arrive.

An unexpected prompt means somebody already has your password.

  • Deny it, every time.
  • Change your password immediately.
  • Report it, even at 2am.

The report wording

Copy this wording
I am receiving repeated multi-factor approval prompts that I did not trigger. I have denied all of them and changed my password from a different device. The prompts started at approximately [TIME] on [DATE]. Please check for sign-in attempts on my account.
Send this to your security contact, whatever the hour.
Why this works
  • It states clearly you denied them, which matters.
  • The time window is what they need to find the attempts.
  • It confirms the password was changed elsewhere, not on a possibly compromised device.
Losing your phone

Before it happens

Before you lose the phone

If it is lost or stolen

  1. Report the loss to IT before you report it anywhere else.
  2. Change your work password from a different device.
  3. Ask for your authenticator registration to be reset.
  4. Check your account's recent sign-in activity once you are back in.
Copy this prompt
My phone with my authenticator app has been lost. Please reset my multi-factor registration and check for any sign-in attempts since [TIME] on [DATE]. I have already changed my password from a different device.
Send this straight away. Speed matters far more than tidiness.
Do this now

Check your own setup

Do this now: check your setup

  1. Open your work account's security settings.
  2. Check which second factor is registered against your name.
  3. If it is SMS and an app is offered, switch to the app.
  4. Find your backup codes and store them away from your phone.
Copy this prompt
Explain in plain English, for someone with no technical background, the difference between receiving a code by SMS, using an authenticator app, and using a passkey. Six lines maximum. No recommendations about specific products.
Useful if you want to understand the choice before making it.

Four habits worth having

Five mistakes people really make

1
Approving a prompt to make it stop
That tap is the only thing the attacker could not do without you.
2
Reading a code to somebody who rang
The call is the attack. Nobody legitimate has ever needed your code.
3
Turning MFA off because it is inconvenient
Seconds of friction against the most common attack there is.
4
Storing backup codes on the same phone
When the phone is lost, both factors are lost with it.
5
Assuming a denied prompt needs no action
An unexpected prompt means your password is already known. Change it.

An unexpected prompt means what?

Did I just try to log in, in the last minute?
Yes

Approve it

You triggered it, it arrived immediately, and it matches what you are doing. That is the only situation in which approval is correct.

No

Deny and act

Somebody has your password and is using it right now. Deny, change the password from another device, and report it whatever the time.

The MFA rule

Approve only what you started. An unexpected prompt is a warning, not an annoyance.

It is telling you, in real time, that your password is already in somebody else's hands.

  • You started it — approve.
  • You did not — deny and change.
  • Someone asks for the code — report.

Glossary

Words used in this module

Multi-factor authentication
Proving who you are with two different kinds of proof: something you know, have or are — not a password alone.
Authenticator app
An app showing a rotating code, tied to the device rather than to a phone number.
Passkey
A modern factor tied to the real website address, so it cannot be used on a fake page.
MFA fatigue
Flooding you with approval prompts until you tap one to stop the noise.
Backup codes
One-time codes for when you lose your phone. Stored away from that phone.
One-time code
A short code proving it is you. Never shared with anyone, for any reason.
